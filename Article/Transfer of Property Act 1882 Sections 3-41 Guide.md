# Transfer of Property Act 1882 Sections 3 to 41: A Complete Guide for Law Students

Picture this exam scenario: a question combines Section 41 ostensible ownership with the definition of "notice" under Section 3. The student who memorised each provision in isolation cannot connect them and loses marks. The **Transfer of Property Act 1882** Sections 3 to 41 are not 39 isolated definitions and rules. They form a single logical chain governing inter vivos transfers of property in India.

The TPA 1882 excludes testamentary transfers (wills) and succession by operation of law. Sections 3 through 41 cover definitions, general principles, conditions and restraints, types of interests, election, apportionment, and protective doctrines. Understanding how each section feeds into the next is the difference between reproducing law and applying it.

What follows is a structured walkthrough from Section 3's foundational definitions through to the doctrine of ostensible ownership under Section 41, with landmark cases and practical exam implications at every step. Each section includes the statutory provision, relevant case law, and actionable exam strategies.

## Section 3 TPA: Seven Key Definitions Every Law Student Must Know

Section 3 is the gateway to the entire Act. Misunderstanding any one of these seven definitions cascades into errors throughout your answers.

### Immovable Property

The TPA does not positively define **immovable property**. It defines it by exclusion: standing timber, growing crops, and grass are not immovable property. The General Clauses Act 1897 includes land, benefits arising out of land, and things attached to the earth. The Registration Act 1908 adds a further layer. The TPA definition is the narrowest of the three.

### Instrument

An **instrument** under TPA means any non-testamentary instrument. Wills fall outside TPA entirely.

### Attested

**Attestation** requires two or more witnesses. Each must have seen the executant sign or received a personal acknowledgement of the signature. In *Shamu Patter v. Abdul Khadar* (Madras HC), the court clarified that both witnesses need not be present simultaneously and cannot be the transferring parties themselves. This definition is critical for gift deeds (Section 123) and mortgage deeds (Section 59).

### Registered

A document is **registered** when it complies with the Indian Registration Act 1908. Registration creates a public record and operates as a form of notice.

### Attached to the Earth

Section 3 identifies three categories: (1) things rooted in the earth (trees, shrubs), (2) things embedded in the earth (walls, buildings), and (3) things attached to what is so embedded (doors, windows fixed to a building).

### Actionable Claim

An **actionable claim** is a claim to any unsecured debt or to any beneficial interest in movable property not in the claimant's possession. It is narrower than the English "chose in action." Actionable claims are transferable under Sections 130 to 137.

### Notice

**Notice** under Section 3 takes three forms. Actual notice: direct personal knowledge of a fact. Constructive notice: knowledge imputed where reasonable inquiry would have revealed the fact. Registration as notice: a registered document gives constructive notice to the world. This concept reappears in Section 52 (lis pendens), though the two operate on different principles.

## General Rules of Transfer Under Sections 5 to 9 TPA

A right to sue for damages cannot be transferred, even if the underlying claim has significant monetary value. This prohibition under Section 6 surprises students who assume anything with economic value is transferable. Sections 5 through 9 establish the foundational rules governing what can be transferred, by whom, and how.

### Section 5: What Is a Transfer?

Section 5 defines **transfer of property** as an act by which a living person conveys property, in present or in future, to one or more other living persons. "Living person" includes companies and associations. Testamentary transfers and transfers by operation of law are excluded.

### Section 6: What Cannot Be Transferred

Section 6 lists non-transferable properties and interests:

- **Spes successionis**: the mere chance of an heir apparent succeeding to an estate.
- A **mere right to sue** for damages.
- A **public office** or the salary of a public officer.
- Stipends allowed to military and naval officers, and pensions.
- A right to **future maintenance**, unless already determined.
- An **easement** apart from the dominant heritage.
- An interest **restricted** to the owner personally.

Exam mnemonic: expectations (spes successionis), personal rights (right to sue, public office), state benefits (stipends, pensions), property-linked restrictions (easements, restricted interests, future maintenance).

### Section 7: Competency to Transfer

Every person **competent to contract** and entitled to transferable property can transfer it (Section 11, Indian Contract Act 1872: major, sound mind, not disqualified). A minor cannot be a transferor but can be a transferee.

### Section 8: Operation of Transfer

Section 8 embodies **nemo dat quod non habet**: no one can give what they do not have. A transfer passes all the interest the transferor is capable of passing, together with attached rights, easements, rents, and profits. This principle is later qualified by Section 41 (ostensible ownership).

### Section 9: Oral vs. Written Transfer

Immovable property valued under one hundred rupees may be transferred without writing. Property valued at one hundred rupees or more requires a **registered instrument**.

## Conditions and Restraints on Transfer: Sections 10 to 18

Can a father gift a house to his son but prohibit him from ever selling it? Sections 10 to 18 draw the line: the law respects the transferor's intent but will not let it destroy ownership itself.

### Section 10: Absolute Restraint on Alienation

A condition **absolutely restraining alienation** is void; the transfer remains valid but the condition falls away. In *Rosher v. Rosher*, even a protective motive did not save an absolute restraint.

### Section 11: Repugnant Conditions

A condition **repugnant** to the interest created is void. If A transfers a house to B absolutely but stipulates B can never lease it, the stipulation fails because it contradicts the absolute transfer.

### Section 12: Partial Restraints

**Partial restraints** on enjoyment may be valid. A condition restricting alienation to a particular class or for a limited time may be upheld if it does not amount to an absolute prohibition.

### Sections 13 and 14: Transfers for Unborn Persons and the Rule Against Perpetuities

Section 13 permits a transfer for the benefit of an **unborn person**, but only if a prior life interest is given to a living person, and the unborn person receives the property absolutely.

Section 14 contains the Indian **rule against perpetuities**: no transfer can create an interest that does not vest within the lifetime of one or more living persons plus the minority of the ultimate beneficiary. Indian law substitutes "minority" (age 18) for the English 21-year period.

### Sections 16 to 18: Conditions Precedent and Subsequent

A **condition precedent** (Section 16) must be fulfilled before the transferee takes. Breach of a **condition subsequent** (Section 17) divests the interest. Section 18 exempts transfers in perpetuity for **public benefit** (charitable or religious purposes) from the perpetuity rule.

## Vested Interest vs Contingent Interest: Sections 19 to 28 Explained

Consider this exam problem: "A transfers property to B for life, remainder to C if C marries D. C dies unmarried. Does C's heir get anything?" The answer turns entirely on whether C's interest was vested or contingent.

### Section 19: Vested Interest

A **vested interest** arises on the execution of the transfer itself, even though enjoyment is postponed. The interest is not conditional on any uncertain event. It is transferable and heritable from the moment of creation.

In *K. Muniswamy v. K. Venkataswamy* (AIR 1991 SC 1201), the Supreme Court held that a vested interest does not lapse on the death of the beneficiary before actual enjoyment. The interest passes to the beneficiary's heirs.

### Section 21: Contingent Interest

A **contingent interest** depends on the happening of an event that may or may not occur. Until the contingency is fulfilled, the interest does not vest. In the exam problem, C's interest is contingent on marrying D. C died unmarried, so C's heirs take nothing.

### Comparison Table: Vested vs. Contingent Interest

| Feature | Vested Interest (Section 19) | Contingent Interest (Section 21) |
|---|---|---|
| When right is created | On execution of transfer | Only when contingency is fulfilled |
| Transferability | Freely transferable | Not transferable inter vivos |
| Devolution on death | Passes to heirs | Does not pass to heirs if contingency unfulfilled |
| Defeasibility | May be subject to divestment | Fails entirely if contingency not met |

### Sections 22, 24, and 25

Section 22: transfers to **members of a class** who satisfy a particular condition. Only those who fulfil the condition take the interest.

Section 24: where a **prior interest fails**, the question is whether the subsequent interest accelerates. The answer depends on the transferor's intention.

Section 25: **conditional interests** are divested upon fulfilment of a condition subsequent. If A transfers to B but provides that B's interest ceases if B moves abroad, B's interest is divested if the condition is fulfilled.

## Doctrine of Election Under Sections 35 and 36 TPA

A, in a transfer document, gifts B's house to C and simultaneously gives B Rs 5 lakh. B wants the money and the house. The doctrine of election says no.

### Section 35: The Principle

The **doctrine of election** applies where a transfer purports to deal with property the transferor does not own. The true owner must choose: accept the benefit conferred under the instrument, or retain their own property. They cannot do both.

If the true owner elects to retain their property, they must compensate the disappointed transferee. Election may be express or implied by conduct. The structure involves three parties: the transferor (A), the true owner (B), and the intended beneficiary (C).

### Section 36: Exception

No election is required where the transferor actually has the **power to transfer** the property. If A had authority to deal with B's house (e.g., as B's agent), Section 35 does not apply.

In *Nawab Khwaja Muhammad Khan v. Nawab Husaini Begam* ((1910) 37 IA 152), the Privy Council laid down the foundational principles of election. The court clarified that election is rooted in equity: a person who accepts a benefit under an instrument must also bear its burdens.

Election must be distinguished from **estoppel**. Election is a choice between two inconsistent rights under the same instrument. Estoppel prevents a person from denying a prior representation.

## Apportionment and Obligation Rules: Sections 37 to 40 TPA

These four sections collapse into one principle: burdens follow benefits. When property changes hands, the obligations attached to it travel with it.

### Section 37: Liability Follows Income

Where property is transferred, the **liability** attached to its income follows the transfer. The transferee who receives rental income also assumes the connected obligations.

### Section 38: Apportionment of Periodical Payments

**Periodical payments** such as rent and profits are divided proportionally based on time or extent of interest. If a landlord sells property on the 15th of a month for which rent has been paid, the rent is apportioned between seller and buyer.

### Section 39: Transferor's Duty to Protect Transferee

Where a third party is entitled to the property, the **transferor is bound** to protect the transferee from such claims.

### Section 40: Obligation Annexed to Ownership

An obligation annexed to **ownership of property** runs with the land and binds the transferee. Classic example: a landlord sells property mid-lease. The tenant's rights under the lease are enforceable against the new owner, who cannot evict the tenant merely because they were not party to the original lease.

## Section 41 TPA and the Doctrine of Ostensible Ownership

Indian law can give a buyer better title than the seller actually had. This directly overrides the nemo dat principle from Section 8 and is one of the most frequently examined TPA provisions.

### The Rule

Section 41 protects a transferee where the **ostensible owner** transfers property for consideration. Three essentials: (1) the true owner consented, expressly or impliedly, to the transferor posing as owner, (2) the transferee acted in good faith after taking reasonable care, (3) the transfer is for consideration, not a gift.

### The Leading Case

In *Jumma Masjid Mercara v. Kodimaniandra Deviah* (AIR 1962 SC 847), the Supreme Court held that the true owner who allows another to hold themselves out as owner cannot defeat the title of a bona fide purchaser for value. The burden falls on the true owner to explain why they permitted the ostensible ownership.

### Distinctions and Connections

Section 41 must be distinguished from the **Benami Transactions (Prohibition) Act**, which prohibits and voids transactions where property is held in one person's name but paid for by another.

Section 52 (lis pendens) prevents transfers during pending litigation from affecting the rights of parties to the suit. It operates on public policy, not notice under Section 3. Section 53A (part performance) protects a transferee who has taken possession and performed their part of the contract, but it is strictly a **shield** (defence against eviction), not a **sword** (cause of action for specific performance).

## FAQ: Transfer of Property Act Sections 3 to 41

**Q1: What is the difference between vested interest and contingent interest under TPA?**

A vested interest (Section 19) arises on execution, with enjoyment postponed; it is transferable and heritable. A contingent interest (Section 21) depends on an uncertain event and is neither transferable inter vivos nor heritable if unfulfilled.

**Q2: Can a condition absolutely restraining alienation be valid under TPA?**

No. Section 10 voids such conditions. The transfer stands, but the restraint falls. Even protective motives cannot save it (*Rosher v. Rosher*).

**Q3: What are the requirements for Section 41 protection (ostensible ownership)?**

Three conditions: ostensible ownership with the true owner's consent, good faith after reasonable care by the transferee, and transfer for consideration. Leading authority: *Jumma Masjid Mercara v. Kodimaniandra Deviah* (AIR 1962 SC 847).

**Q4: Is the doctrine of lis pendens the same as notice under Section 3?**

No. Lis pendens (Section 52) operates independently of notice. It is founded on public policy, not knowledge. A transfer during suit pendency is affected regardless of the transferee's awareness.

**Q5: Is Section 53A (part performance) an offensive or defensive provision?**

Strictly a defence. A transferee cannot sue for specific performance under Section 53A alone, but can use it to resist eviction after taking possession and performing their part of the contract.
