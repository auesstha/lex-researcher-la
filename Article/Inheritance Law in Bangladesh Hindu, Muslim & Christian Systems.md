# Inheritance Law in Bangladesh: How Hindu, Muslim, and Christian Succession Systems Diverge

Bangladesh has no single inheritance statute. At least four distinct legal regimes govern succession: Hanafi fiqh, Dayabhaga Hindu law, the Succession Act 1925, and uncodified Buddhist customary law. None acknowledge the others' existence. The deceased's religion, not citizenship or domicile, determines which rules apply. **Inheritance law Bangladesh Hindu Muslim Christian** systems operate as parallel frameworks within a single jurisdiction, producing radically different outcomes for identically situated families.

A Muslim widow inherits one-eighth of her husband's estate when children survive. A Christian widow inherits one-third. A Hindu widow receives a life interest she cannot sell. These are not edge cases. They are the default outcomes of three systems that Bangladesh applies simultaneously, with no harmonizing statute bridging the gaps.

This article maps each framework's mechanics, traces the fissures that open when families cross religious boundaries, and examines reform proposals that have generated fierce political opposition. Bangladesh's current inheritance architecture permits legal pluralism but fails the equality guarantee in its own Constitution (Articles 27 and 28).

## Personal Law Pluralism: Why Religion Determines Inheritance in Bangladesh

British India codified Hindu, Muslim, and Christian succession as separate statutory and customary regimes. Bangladesh preserved this structure after independence in 1971 and has made no significant move toward unification since. A citizen's religion, not nationality or domicile, determines which **inheritance law Bangladesh Hindu Muslim Christian** rules apply to their estate.

India enacted the Hindu Succession Act 1956, codifying and partially unifying Hindu inheritance law, then amended it in 2005 to grant daughters coparcenary rights. Bangladesh made no equivalent reform. Dayabhaga Hindu law remains uncodified customary law, applied by courts on the basis of treatise authority and precedent rather than statute.

The controlling instruments are sparse. The Muslim Personal Law (Shariat) Application Act 1937 directs Muslims to Hanafi fiqh for inheritance. The Succession Act 1925, Part V, governs Christian intestate succession. No statute governs Hindu succession at all. The Bangladesh Constitution, Article 28, guarantees equality and prohibits discrimination on grounds of religion or sex, yet personal law has been treated as an exception, and no successful constitutional challenge has displaced any religious inheritance regime to date.

A family with a Hindu father and Muslim mother illustrates the problem. The father's estate follows Dayabhaga; the mother's estate follows Hanafi faraid. The children hold different entitlements under each parent's regime. No statute addresses this intersection, and no conflicts-of-law principle resolves it.

## Muslim Inheritance Under Hanafi Law: Fixed Shares, Residuaries, and the MFLO Amendment

Muslim inheritance in Bangladesh follows a precise mathematical system rooted in Hanafi fiqh, modified by the Muslim Family Laws Ordinance 1961. Bangladesh courts have upheld this modification even as Pakistan's courts rejected it.

### The Faraid Structure

Hanafi faraid recognizes twelve Quranic heirs entitled to fixed shares (fard). Before distribution, the estate must satisfy funeral expenses, debts (including unpaid denmohor), and bequests (wasiyyah), capped at one-third of the net estate and barred from benefiting an existing heir without the other heirs' consent.

The fixed shares follow specific rules. A sole daughter receives one-half; two or more daughters share two-thirds. When a son survives alongside a daughter, both become residuary heirs (asaba), and the son takes double the daughter's share (2:1 ratio). A widow receives one-eighth when children survive, one-quarter without. A husband receives one-quarter with children, one-half without. The mother takes one-sixth with children, one-third otherwise. The father receives one-sixth when children survive and takes the residue if only daughters survive.

A worked example demonstrates the Hanafi doctrine of awl (proportional reduction). A Muslim man dies survived by his wife, two daughters, mother, and a full sister. The wife's share is 1/8 (3/24), the two daughters share 2/3 (16/24), the mother takes 1/6 (4/24), and the full sister takes 1/2 (12/24). The total is 35/24, exceeding the estate. Awl reduces all shares proportionally: each fraction's denominator increases from 24 to 35. The wife receives 3/35 (8.57%), the daughters share 16/35 (45.71%), the mother receives 4/35 (11.43%), and the sister receives 12/35 (34.29%).

### The MFLO Amendment

The Muslim Family Laws Ordinance 1961, s 4, introduced the doctrine of representation for orphaned grandchildren. If a son or daughter predeceases the propositus, that child's children inherit the share their parent would have received. Pakistan's Federal Shariat Court declared s 4 repugnant to Islamic injunctions in 2000. Bangladesh courts have not followed Pakistan's position, and s 4 remains settled law.

The Appellate Division confirmed the operative date for s 4 claims in *Sheikh Ibrahim v Nazma Begum*, 44 DLR (AD) 276. The court held that the date of death of the predeceased parent is immaterial; what governs is the date the succession opens, meaning the date of the propositus's own death. *Khandaker Abdul Hannan v Sayara Begum*, 56 DLR (AD) 141 (2004), is the leading Appellate Division authority on calculating the grandchildren's share once s 4 is triggered.

Practitioners should note that s 4 is frequently overlooked. Warish certificates issued by local government offices and online faraiz calculators routinely omit orphaned grandchildren's shares, producing incorrect distributions. The National Legal Aid Services Organization (NLASO) recovered Tk 42 crore in inheritance and maintenance claims through alternative dispute resolution in 2025, indicating the scale of enforcement gaps.

Three exclusion rules apply: a non-Muslim heir cannot inherit from a Muslim's estate, a murderer of the deceased is excluded, and apostasy (riddah) extinguishes inheritance rights in both directions.

## Hindu Inheritance Under the Dayabhaga School: How Bengal's System Differs from the Rest of South Asia

In most of South Asia, a Hindu son acquires inheritance rights at birth. In Bangladesh, he acquires nothing until his father dies. This single difference reshapes the entire framework.

### The Dayabhaga Mechanism

The Dayabhaga, attributed to Jimutavahana (12th century), determines inheritance by spiritual efficacy: the capacity to offer pinda (funeral oblations). The son ranks first because he offers the most efficacious pinda. Property does not vest in a son by birth. The father holds absolute ownership during his lifetime. There is no coparcenary in the Mitakshara sense.

Under Mitakshara, followed in most of India, sons acquire a birth-right in ancestral property, forming a coparcenary that limits the father's power to alienate. India codified both schools via the Hindu Succession Act 1956, then its 2005 amendment extended coparcenary rights to daughters. Bangladesh has no equivalent statute. Dayabhaga applies as uncodified customary law, interpreted through Jimutavahana's treatise, later commentaries, and judicial precedent.

### Testamentary Freedom and Gender

The Dayabhaga father's absolute ownership grants unlimited testamentary freedom. He can will his entire estate to a single child, a stranger, or a charity. No forced heirship rule constrains him. Muslim law, by contrast, caps bequests at one-third of the net estate. Mitakshara sons can challenge alienation of coparcenary property. The Dayabhaga heir has no equivalent recourse.

On intestacy, the gender consequences are severe. Married daughters are excluded from the father's intestate estate entirely under traditional Dayabhaga. Unmarried daughters inherit only after sons. The Hindu Women's Rights to Property Act 1937 (Act XVIII of 1937) grants the widow a share equivalent to a son's share, but as a life interest only, with no power to alienate. The High Court Division resolved a long-standing ambiguity about the Act's scope in *Jyotindra Nath Mondal v Gouri Dasi*, Civil Revision No. 2110 of 2004 (High Court Division, 2020): the widow's life interest under the 1937 Act extends to agricultural land. Courts had previously applied the Act inconsistently to agricultural holdings; this ruling settled the position, though the expansion still falls short of absolute ownership.

The Appellate Division confirmed the Dayabhaga position on stridhan (women's exclusive property) in an unreported judgment of 11 December 2022 (arising in *Elokeshi v Estate of Rukkhini Dashi*). The court held that a daughter's daughter inherits her grandmother's stridhan absolutely under Dayabhaga, applying the principle that daughters inherit stridhan in the same manner as sons inherit their father's property. The court reaffirmed that the doctrine of religious efficacy (pinda capacity) governs the entire Dayabhaga succession hierarchy. An earlier High Court Division decision, *Kalpana Das Gupta v Government of Bangladesh*, 40 DLR 373, addressed the discriminatory treatment of Hindu females in succession to a deceased father's property in the context of constitutional rights, signaling that courts have long recognized the tension between Dayabhaga rules and constitutional equality guarantees even without resolving it by statute.

### A Concrete Distribution

A Hindu man dies intestate in Dhaka, survived by his wife, two sons, and a married daughter. The wife takes a share equal to one son's, but as a life interest. Each son takes one-third of the estate in full ownership. The married daughter takes nothing. The wife's life interest terminates on her death or remarriage, reverting to the sons.

The doctrinal consequence is stark: marriage operates as a disinheritance mechanism for Hindu daughters. Reform proposals (discussed in Section 9) target this exclusion as the central injustice of Dayabhaga succession.

## Christian Inheritance Under the Succession Act 1925: Equal Shares and Mandatory Probate

Christian **inheritance law Bangladesh Hindu Muslim Christian** systems contrast most sharply on gender. Sons and daughters receive identical shares under the Succession Act 1925. The governing statute is explicit and leaves little to judicial interpretation.

### The Statutory Framework

The Succession Act 1925, Part V (Sections 32 through 48), governs Christian intestate succession. Section 33 gives the widow one-third when lineal descendants survive. Section 34 grants the widow one-half if no lineal descendants but other kindred exist, and the entire estate if no kindred survive. Section 35 distributes the remainder equally among children, with no gender distinction and no exclusion of married daughters.

If a child predeceases the intestate, that child's share passes by representation to their own children under Sections 37 through 40. The extended family hierarchy (Sections 42 through 48) is fully codified: father first, then mother and siblings equally, then mother alone, then siblings alone.

### Probate, Testamentary Freedom, and Procedural Requirements

Christians possess full testamentary freedom under Part VI. A Christian testator can will the entire estate to anyone. No forced heirship rule applies. A will is revoked by subsequent marriage.

The procedural burden on Christians is heavier than on Muslims or Hindus. Section 222 makes probate mandatory for Christian wills. For intestate estates, letters of administration from the District Judge's court are standard. Section 212(2) exempts Bangladesh Christians from mandatory letters of administration to establish rights in court, but probate remains necessary for testate estates.

Muslims mutate land records through warish certificates without court proceedings. Hindus use revenue office procedures based on customary law. The Christian heir's path runs through the courts, adding cost and delay that other communities avoid.

## Buddhist Succession: The Uncodified Gap in Bangladesh's Inheritance Framework

No statute governs inheritance for Bangladesh's approximately one million Buddhists.

The Succession Act 1925, s 29, excludes Buddhists from Part V's intestacy provisions. In the Chittagong Hill Tracts, Buddhist communities (Chakma, Marma, Tanchangya) follow tribal customary law that varies by community and is administered partly through traditional institutions. Plains Buddhists outside the Hill Tracts may find courts applying Hindu Dayabhaga principles by judicial analogy, though this approach lacks clear statutory or precedential authority.

The Special Marriage Act 1872 compounds the problem. Section 24 directs Buddhists marrying under the Act to the Succession Act 1925 for inheritance. But s 29 of that Act excludes Buddhists from Part V. The result is a circular gap: one statute points to another that refuses to apply. No reported decision has resolved this contradiction.

## Inter-Religious Inheritance: Conversion, Mixed Marriages, and the Gaps Between Systems

A Hindu who converts to Islam may lose inheritance rights under both systems simultaneously, falling into a gap no statute addresses.

### Conversion Out of Hinduism

The Appellate Division confirmed this result in *Palash Chandra Saha v Shimul Rani Saha* (2020). A Hindu who converts loses all rights to inherit from their Hindu family's estate. The reasoning follows Dayabhaga's foundation: inheritance depends on the capacity to offer pinda. A convert cannot perform Hindu funeral rites and therefore loses the spiritual efficacy that grounds inheritance entitlement.

In India, the Caste Disabilities Removal Act 1850 preserves the convert's inheritance rights. Bangladesh repealed this Act in 1973. Conversion out of Hinduism therefore operates as complete disinheritance from the Hindu family's estate.

### Muslim Apostasy and the Hanafi Position

An apostate (murtadd) loses the right to inherit from Muslim relatives' estates under Hanafi law. Classical Hanafi doctrine holds that property acquired before apostasy passes to Muslim heirs, while property acquired after apostasy may fall into a legal void. No recent Appellate Division authority clarifies the practical application of these rules in Bangladesh.

### The Special Marriage Act 1872 and Its Limitations

The Special Marriage Act 1872 was designed for inter-religious marriages, but its design is defective. Section 2 requires renunciation of religious identity for most couples. Section 24 provides a succession framework only for Hindus, Buddhists, Sikhs, and Jains who marry under it, directing them to the Succession Act 1925. For Muslims, Christians, Parsis, and Jews, s 24 provides no direction. The Dhaka Tribune identified this void in 2018, and no legislative correction has followed.

### Mixed-Religion Families Under Classical Doctrine

When a Muslim man dies intestate survived by a Christian wife, Hanafi law governs his estate. Classical Hanafi doctrine excludes non-Muslim heirs. The Christian wife inherits nothing. She has no claim under the Succession Act 1925 either, because that Act governs estates of Christian decedents, not claims by Christian heirs against Muslim estates.

The asymmetry runs one direction only. The Succession Act does not exclude Muslim heirs from a Christian decedent's estate. A Muslim husband can inherit from his Christian wife's estate. Hanafi doctrine's religion-based exclusion has no counterpart in the Succession Act.

### A Compound Example

A Hindu woman converts to Islam, marries a Muslim man, and the husband dies. She inherits from her Muslim husband's estate as a Muslim widow under Hanafi faraid (one-eighth if children survive, one-quarter otherwise). From her Hindu father's estate, she inherits nothing, because *Palash Chandra Saha* extinguished her Hindu inheritance rights upon conversion, and the 1973 repeal of the Caste Disabilities Removal Act closed the only statutory path that might have preserved them. Courts resolve these cross-system disputes case by case, with no harmonizing principle.

## Comparative Share Analysis: How the Same Family Inherits Differently Under Each System

A single scenario applied across all three systems: a husband dies intestate, survived by his wife, one son, and one daughter.

**Muslim (Hanafi faraid).** The wife takes one-eighth (12.5%). The son and daughter inherit the remaining seven-eighths as residuary heirs in a 2:1 ratio. The son receives 7/12 of the total estate (58.33%). The daughter receives 7/24 (29.17%). Controlling authority: Hanafi fiqh as applied through the Muslim Personal Law (Shariat) Application Act 1937.

**Hindu (Dayabhaga).** The wife takes a share equal to the son's, but as a life interest only under the Hindu Women's Rights to Property Act 1937. If the daughter is married, she is excluded entirely. The son takes his share in full ownership. On the wife's death, her share reverts to the son.

**Christian (Succession Act 1925).** The wife takes one-third under s 33. The son and daughter split the remaining two-thirds equally under s 35, each receiving one-third of the total estate. No gender distinction. No life-interest limitation.

Three structural divergences emerge. Gender equality: the Christian system treats sons and daughters identically; the Muslim system applies a 2:1 ratio; the Hindu system excludes married daughters. Spousal protection: the Christian widow receives one-third in full ownership; the Muslim widow receives one-eighth; the Hindu widow receives an inalienable life interest. Testamentary override: the Hindu and Christian testator enjoy unlimited freedom; the Muslim testator is capped at one-third.

## The Reform Debate: Uniform Succession Law and the Politics of Personal Law in Bangladesh

In April 2025, the Women's Affairs Reform Commission submitted its report containing 443 proposals, including a uniform family law granting equal shares to sons and daughters across all religions.

### Opposition

Hefazat-e-Islam organized a Grand Rally on 3 May 2025, demanding withdrawal of the Commission's report. The Bangladesh National Hindu Grand Alliance also opposed changes. Gobinda Chandra Pramanik of the Grand Alliance argued that existing law "adequately protects Hindu women through maintenance rights and property supervision." A 2011 proposal for gender-equal inheritance was withdrawn under pressure from Jamaat-e-Islam. The pattern is consistent: proposals surface, religious organizations mobilize, and the government retreats.

### Support

BLAST, Ain o Salish Kendra, Manusher Jonno Foundation, and Bangladesh Mahila Parishad have supported reform. A Citizens' Initiative drafted a bill for Hindu succession proposing equal inheritance regardless of gender, a four-tier heir classification, full ownership rights for widows, and a cap on testamentary bequests at one-half of inherited property.

Advocate Deepti Sikder of Bangladesh Mohila Parishad has called for abolishing the "discriminatory" Muslim Family Laws Ordinance 1961 and introducing a unified family code. Rana Das Gupta of the Hindu Buddhist Christian Unity Council has called existing Hindu **inheritance law** "discriminatory" against women.

### The Constitutional Argument and Structural Obstacles

BLAST's Tajul Islam has argued that current Hindu inheritance law violates Articles 27 and 28 of the Bangladesh Constitution. Sultan, Roy Nath, and colleagues at BRAC Institute and IDS published a 2025 working paper analyzing how backlash against reform manifests from three sources: the state, the Hindu community itself, and Hindu religious leaders.

Former Law Minister Anisul Huq stated: "We cannot pass a law, especially the Hindu property inheritance law, for the people who will not accept it." Conservative leaders within each community hold an effective veto over legislation that would benefit the women within those communities. Whether the 2025 Commission's proposals survive this dynamic, or follow the 2011 precedent into withdrawal, remains the defining open question for **inheritance law Bangladesh Hindu Muslim Christian** reform.

## Practical Procedures: Succession Certificates, Partition Suits, and Estate Administration

Knowing your inheritance share is only the first step. Realizing it requires navigating procedures that vary by religion and property type.

**Succession certificates.** The Succession Act 1925, Part X, covers movable property and is available to heirs of all religions. Application is made to the District Judge. A succession certificate authorizes collection of debts and securities due to the deceased but does not establish ownership of immovable property.

**Probate and letters of administration.** Probate is mandatory for Christians under s 222 when a will exists. Letters of administration serve intestate Christian estates. Muslims and Hindus generally do not require either for property transfers, though they may seek them for evidentiary purposes.

**Partition suits.** When co-heirs cannot agree on division, any heir may file under the Partition Act 1893. These suits are common in Hindu families where joint possession continues for generations. Court-ordered partition involves surveying, valuation, and allocation, routinely taking years. The Appellate Division confirmed in *Abdul Mumin alias Tanu Miah v Mahfujur Rahman*, 55 DLR (AD) 93 (2003), that co-sharers by inheritance in an undivided holding retain co-sharer status until legal partition occurs, whether by court decree or registered instrument. A pending partition suit does not itself constitute severance of title.

**Land mutation.** Muslims prove heirship through warish certificates. Hindus establish heirship through Dayabhaga customary rules. Christians present probate or letters of administration. The process is administrative, but disputes over heirship can escalate to civil court.

## Key Statutes and Cases Governing Inheritance Law in Bangladesh

2. **Muslim Family Laws Ordinance 1961, s 4.** Grants orphaned grandchildren their predeceased parent's share. Settled in Bangladesh; declared repugnant in Pakistan (2000).

3. **Succession Act 1925, Part V (ss 32-48).** Governs Christian intestate succession with equal shares for sons and daughters.

4. **Succession Act 1925, s 29.** Excludes Hindus, Buddhists, Sikhs, and Jains from Part V.

5. **Hindu Women's Rights to Property Act 1937.** Grants Hindu widow a life interest equivalent to one son's share.

6. **Special Marriage Act 1872, ss 2 and 24.** Requires religious renunciation for most inter-religious marriages. Provides succession rules only for Hindu, Buddhist, Sikh, and Jain parties.

7. **Caste Disabilities Removal Act 1850.** Repealed in Bangladesh (1973). Would have preserved Hindu converts' inheritance rights.

8. ***Sheikh Ibrahim v Nazma Begum*, Appellate Division, 44 DLR (AD) 276.** The operative date for MFLO 1961 s 4 claims is the date the propositus's succession opens, not the date of death of the predeceased parent.

9. ***Khandaker Abdul Hannan v Sayara Begum*, Appellate Division, 56 DLR (AD) 141 (2004).** Leading authority on calculating orphaned grandchildren's share under MFLO 1961 s 4.

10. ***Jyotindra Nath Mondal v Gouri Dasi*, High Court Division, Civil Revision No. 2110 of 2004 (2020).** Hindu widow's life interest under the Hindu Women's Rights to Property Act 1937 extends to agricultural land.

11. ***Kalpana Das Gupta v Government of Bangladesh*, High Court Division, 40 DLR 373.** Addressed discriminatory treatment of Hindu females in succession to a deceased father's property and its tension with constitutional equality provisions.

12. ***Elokeshi v Estate of Rukkhini Dashi*, Appellate Division, 11 December 2022.** A daughter's daughter inherits grandmother's stridhan absolutely under Dayabhaga; reaffirmed the doctrine of religious efficacy as the governing principle of Dayabhaga succession.

13. ***Abdul Mumin alias Tanu Miah v Mahfujur Rahman*, Appellate Division, 55 DLR (AD) 93 (2003).** Co-sharer status by inheritance persists until legal partition by court decree or registered instrument; a pending partition suit does not constitute severance of title.

14. ***Palash Chandra Saha v Shimul Rani Saha*, Appellate Division, 2020.** Conversion out of Hinduism extinguishes inheritance rights under Dayabhaga; the Caste Disabilities Removal Act 1850 has no application in Bangladesh.

## Frequently Asked Questions About Inheritance Law in Bangladesh

### Can a Muslim in Bangladesh disinherit an heir by will?

No. Hanafi law caps bequests at one-third of the net estate, and bequests to existing Quranic heirs require all other heirs' consent. The fixed shares (fard) prescribed by faraid cannot be overridden by will.

### Do adopted children inherit under any system in Bangladesh?

Muslim personal law does not recognize adoption; an adopted child does not inherit under Hanafi faraid. Under Hindu Dayabhaga, adoption (dattaka) is recognized and an adopted son inherits as a natural son. The Succession Act 1925 governs Christian intestate succession based on "kindred" and "lineal descendants" without expressly distinguishing between natural and adopted children; how Bangladeshi courts treat legally adopted children under that Act has not been settled by appellate authority.

### Does a non-Muslim spouse inherit from a Muslim's estate?

Under classical Hanafi doctrine applied in Bangladesh, no. The religious identity of heir and deceased must match. A Christian wife of a Muslim husband inherits nothing from his estate on intestacy. The Succession Act 1925 does not remedy this gap because it governs Christian decedents' estates, not Muslim estates.

### Can a Hindu father will his entire estate to one child?

Yes. Under Dayabhaga, the father holds absolute ownership and unlimited testamentary freedom. No forced heirship rule constrains him. This power enables complete disinheritance of daughters, which reform advocates identify as a central injustice of the current system.
