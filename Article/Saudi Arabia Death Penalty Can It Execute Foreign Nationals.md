# Can the Government of Saudi Arabia Carry Out the Death Penalty Against a Foreign National?

In 2025, Saudi Arabia executed 356 people. Of those, 188 were foreign nationals, the highest annual figure ever recorded and the first time foreigners constituted a majority of those put to death by the Saudi state. Two hundred and forty of those executions were for drug offenses.

The answer to the title question is direct: yes. Saudi Arabia asserts full territorial jurisdiction over every person on its soil, regardless of nationality. No domestic statute, no bilateral treaty, and no judicial exception limits the state's claimed authority to impose **the death penalty against foreign nationals**. The legal framework permits it. The data confirms it is applied disproportionately against migrant workers from countries with large labor export populations and limited diplomatic leverage.

I examine below the legal basis for these executions, the systematic due process failures that define them, the international law obligations Saudi Arabia is arguably violating, and the practical steps advocates and governments can take. For anyone working in international criminal law, migration policy, or human rights advocacy, the pattern is documented, quantified, and accelerating.

## The Legal Basis for Executing Foreign Nationals in Saudi Arabia

Saudi Arabia's **Basic Law of Governance** (Royal Decree A/90, 1992) establishes Sharia as the constitution and supreme law of the Kingdom, applicable to all persons present on Saudi territory. Article 1 declares the Quran and the Sunnah the country's constitution. Article 36 provides that the state shall provide security for all its residents. Neither provision draws any distinction based on nationality.

The **territorial jurisdiction principle** operates here without qualification. This reflects a foundational rule of international law articulated in *S.S. "Lotus" (France v. Turkey)* (PCIJ, Series A No. 10, Sept. 7, 1927): a state may, as a general rule, exercise criminal jurisdiction over any act committed on its territory, and no rule of international law prohibits it from doing so. Saudi courts apply Sharia as interpreted by Saudi judges, and that jurisdiction extends to every person physically present in the Kingdom. There is no carve-out for foreign defendants, no mandatory consular review mechanism embedded in domestic procedure, and no statutory limitation on the penalties that may be imposed on non-citizens.

Capital offenses in Saudi Arabia fall into two categories. **Hudud** offenses carry fixed Quranic penalties and include crimes such as apostasy, adultery, and highway robbery. **Taazir** offenses carry discretionary penalties determined by judges, and this category includes drug trafficking. The distinction matters enormously. In 2024, 141 of 345 executions fell under Taazir, meaning the sentencing judge had full discretion not to impose death but chose to do so (Reprieve, 2025). For foreign nationals convicted of drug offenses, the death sentence is not compelled by Sharia. It is a judicial choice.

This stands in contrast to jurisdictions that either exempt foreign nationals from capital punishment or mandate consular review before a death sentence can be carried out. Saudi Arabia does neither. Until recent partial reforms, the Kingdom lacked a codified penal code entirely, leaving sentencing almost wholly to individual judicial discretion. The result is a system in which the most severe penalty is available for the broadest range of conduct, applied by judges with the least structural constraint.

## The Scale of Foreign National Executions: Statistics and Patterns

The numbers are not in dispute.

In 2025, Saudi Arabia carried out 356 executions. 188 of those executed were foreign nationals, representing 53% of the total. 240 executions were for drug-related offenses (Reprieve, 2025). In 2024, the Kingdom executed 345 people, 138 of whom were foreign nationals (40%). Seventy-five percent of those executed for drug offenses in 2024 were foreign nationals (Amnesty International, 2025).

The nationality breakdown over the past decade reveals a clear pattern. According to Amnesty International's July 2025 report, the most executed nationalities were Pakistanis (155), Syrians (66), Jordanians (50), Yemenis (39), Egyptians (33), and Nigerians (32). These are nationals from countries with large migrant labor populations in the Gulf, countries whose governments exercise limited diplomatic leverage over Riyadh, and countries whose citizens occupy the lowest rungs of the Saudi labor market.

Drug offenses drive the majority of foreign national executions. This is legally significant because drug trafficking does not meet the threshold of "**most serious crimes**" under international law, a standard that the UN Human Rights Committee has defined as crimes involving intentional killing (General Comment No. 36, UN Doc. CCPR/C/GC/36, 2019). Every drug execution is, under prevailing international legal standards, a prima facie violation of the right to life.

The trend is accelerating. Saudi Arabia executed two people for drug offenses in 2020. In 2025, that number was 240. This escalation occurred despite the Kingdom's 2021 public announcement of judicial reforms that were said to include restricting the death penalty for drug offenses. The gap between that announcement and the execution data is not a matter of interpretation. It is arithmetic.

Wealthy Gulf nationals and Western passport holders are virtually absent from execution statistics. The system's selectivity is structural, not incidental.

## Systematic Due Process Failures in Capital Cases Involving Foreigners

The procedural deficiencies in Saudi capital cases involving foreign nationals are not occasional failures. They are systemic features.

**Capital trials lasting minutes.** Human rights organizations have documented cases in which capital trials were completed in a single court session, with no meaningful opportunity for the defendant to present a defense (Reprieve, 2024). Defendants who do not speak Arabic are regularly tried without interpretation services. They cannot understand the charges, the testimony, or the sentence.

**Denial of legal representation.** Many foreign defendants never meet a lawyer before or during trial. As one family member of an executed migrant worker told Amnesty International: "If we had money and a lawyer, maybe my brother would be alive." Access to legal defense in Saudi capital cases correlates with wealth and connections, not legal right.

**Coerced confessions** form the evidentiary backbone of many capital convictions. Documented patterns include confessions obtained under physical duress, sleep deprivation, threats against family members, and deception about the legal consequences of signing documents in Arabic that defendants cannot read. These confessions are admitted as primary evidence, often the sole evidence, in proceedings where the defendant has no capacity to challenge them.

Saudi Arabia ratified the Convention Against Torture (CAT) in 1997. Article 15 of the CAT states that "each State Party shall ensure that any statement which is established to have been made as a result of torture shall not be invoked as evidence in any proceedings, except against a person accused of torture as evidence that the statement was made." Saudi Arabia is bound by this obligation. Executions carried out on the basis of such confessions are not only prima facie violations of a peremptory norm (jus cogens, per the Vienna Convention on the Law of Treaties, Art. 53, May 23, 1969, 1155 U.N.T.S. 331) but direct breaches of Saudi Arabia's own treaty commitments.

**VCCR violations** are routine. Article 36 of the Vienna Convention on Consular Relations (Apr. 24, 1963, 596 U.N.T.S. 261) requires that when a foreign national is detained, the detaining state must notify the individual of their right to contact their consulate and must inform the consulate of the detention. Saudi Arabia is a party to the VCCR. In October 2026, two Bangladeshi nationals were executed in Mecca with zero consular notification. The Bangladeshi embassy learned of the executions after the fact. This is standard practice, not an isolated incident.

Proceedings in the **Specialized Criminal Court** are closed to observers, family members, and media. Defendants may not learn that their appeal has been denied until they are taken from their cells for execution.

## International Law Violations: VCCR, Arab Charter, and the ICCPR Standard

Saudi Arabia's execution of foreign nationals engages multiple international legal obligations that the Kingdom is arguably, and in some cases demonstrably, violating.

**VCCR Article 36** creates an obligation to notify consulates when foreign nationals are detained and to permit consular access. The ICJ addressed this obligation in two landmark cases. In *LaGrand (Germany v. United States)* (ICJ, 2001 ICJ Rep. 466), the Court held that Article 36 creates individual rights for detained foreign nationals, not merely state-to-state obligations. In *Avena and Other Mexican Nationals (Mexico v. United States)* (ICJ, 2004 ICJ Rep. 12), the Court confirmed that violations of consular notification rights can affect the validity of criminal convictions and required review and reconsideration of sentences.

The most direct precedent in a regional capital case is *Jadhav (India v. Pakistan)* (ICJ, 2019 ICJ Rep. 418). The Court ordered Pakistan to provide consular access to an Indian national facing execution, holding that VCCR Article 36 imposes binding obligations that the detaining state cannot unilaterally disregard. The Court also required Pakistan to review and reconsider the conviction and sentence. The parallels to Saudi Arabia's conduct are direct. The Bangladesh and Ethiopian cases described above represent clear-cut violations of the same obligations the ICJ enforced in *Jadhav*.

**The ICCPR and the "most serious crimes" standard.** Saudi Arabia has not ratified the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights. However, the UN Human Rights Committee's General Comment No. 36 (2019) articulates the prevailing international standard: the death penalty must be restricted to crimes involving intentional killing. Drug offenses are explicitly excluded. While Saudi Arabia is not directly bound by the ICCPR, this standard informs the interpretation of other instruments to which it is bound.

The **Arab Charter on Human Rights** (May 22, 2004) is one such instrument. Saudi Arabia ratified it. Article 6 restricts the death penalty to "the most serious crimes," mirroring the ICCPR's language. The 240 drug executions carried out in 2025 are prima facie inconsistent with Saudi Arabia's own regional treaty obligations under Article 6.

The prohibition on **torture** is a peremptory norm of international law with erga omnes character, meaning every state has a legal interest in its observance, not just the directly injured party. The ICJ confirmed this in *Questions relating to the Obligation to Prosecute or Extradite (Belgium v. Senegal)* (ICJ, 2012 ICJ Rep. 422), holding that the prohibition on torture has "entered into the body of general international law" and that obligations under the CAT are owed to the international community as a whole. Convictions obtained through confessions extracted under duress are tainted at their foundation. When such convictions result in execution, the violation compounds: a life is extinguished on the basis of evidence that Saudi Arabia's own treaty obligations bar from being used.

## The Unequal System: Kafala, Diya, and the Diplomatic Power Gap

The execution of foreign nationals in Saudi Arabia cannot be understood without examining the structural systems that produce their vulnerability.

The **kafala (sponsorship) system** ties a migrant worker's legal status to their employer. Workers cannot change jobs, leave the country, or in many cases access basic services without their sponsor's permission. Research by the Oxford Death Penalty Research Unit (Hoyle, Harry, and Hutton, *Punishment & Society*, 2024) documents how the kafala system creates "migratory dependency" that exposes South Asian workers to coercion, including coercion into drug transport. The Oxford researchers concluded that some individuals convicted as drug traffickers could more accurately be regarded as victims of trafficking. The legal distinction between perpetrator and victim collapses when the accused had no meaningful capacity to refuse.

The **diya (blood money)** system operates in murder (qisas) cases. The victim's family holds the right to forgive the defendant in exchange for financial compensation, sparing the accused from execution. In principle, this applies equally. In practice, Saudi citizens access this remedy up to eight times more easily than migrant workers (Amnesty International, 2024). Migrant workers lack the family networks, community connections, and financial resources to negotiate diya payments.

The diplomatic power gap completes the picture. When Western nationals face execution, high-level political intervention follows. In 2003, UK Prime Minister Tony Blair personally appealed to King Fahd on behalf of British nationals sentenced to death, and they were pardoned. Meanwhile, mass executions of Pakistani, Ethiopian, and Nigerian nationals proceed with minimal diplomatic protest from their home governments.

The result is a two-tier justice system in which nationality, wealth, and diplomatic leverage determine who lives and who dies.

## Case Studies: Ethiopia, Bangladesh, Egypt, and Sri Lanka

Individual cases illustrate what aggregate statistics obscure.

**Ethiopia and Khamis Mushait.** Over 65 Ethiopian migrants were detained, many after being coerced by smugglers to carry khat, a mild stimulant that is legal and widely consumed in Ethiopia. Seventeen were executed in 2026 (Human Rights Watch, April 2026). None received meaningful legal representation. Families reported that consular visits did not occur until years after initial detention. These individuals were, by multiple credible accounts, trafficking victims prosecuted as criminals.

**Bangladesh and Mecca (October 2026).** Two Bangladeshi nationals were executed in Mecca. The Bangladeshi embassy received no prior notification. This constitutes a textbook violation of VCCR Article 36 obligations. The absence of consular notification meant the individuals had no access to whatever limited assistance their government might have provided.

**Egypt (June 2025).** The UN Special Rapporteur on extrajudicial executions issued an urgent appeal concerning 26 Egyptian nationals facing execution for drug offenses. Some had been convicted on the basis of coerced confessions obtained without legal counsel. The appeal went unanswered.

**Sri Lanka (October 2026).** A death sentence against a Sri Lankan national was overturned following sustained diplomatic engagement by the Sri Lankan Foreign Ministry. The sentence was commuted not on the basis of new evidence, but because a government chose to invest political resources in its citizen's survival.

The contrast between Sri Lanka and Bangladesh is instructive. The decisive variable is not legal merit. It is whether a government prioritizes diplomatic engagement on behalf of its detained nationals. When it does, outcomes change. When it does not, its citizens are executed without notice.

## What Advocates Can Do: Practical Recourse and Intervention Strategies

The situation is severe, but it is not without points of intervention.

**Demand consular notification compliance.** Sending-country governments must assert their rights under VCCR Article 36 and file formal diplomatic protests when those rights are violated. Every unnotified execution should generate a documented objection.

**Engage UN Special Procedures.** Urgent appeals can be filed through the Special Rapporteur on extrajudicial executions and the Working Group on Arbitrary Detention. These mechanisms lack enforcement power, but they create a public record and apply reputational pressure that Saudi Arabia, hosting the 2034 FIFA World Cup, is not indifferent to.

**Push bilateral diplomatic channels.** The Sri Lanka case proves that sustained, high-level diplomatic engagement can commute sentences. Advocates should pressure home governments to treat the defense of nationals facing execution as a foreign policy priority.

**Fund Arabic-speaking defense lawyers.** The absence of legal representation is the most immediate procedural failure. Networks of qualified defense attorneys, funded by international legal aid organizations, can be the difference between a death sentence and a lesser penalty.

**Facilitate diya payments in qisas cases.** In 2025, a Kenyan national was freed after a Christian charity paid diya on their behalf. This avenue is limited to murder cases, but where it applies, it is direct and effective.

## The Path Forward: Reform Prospects and Remaining Challenges

In 2021, Saudi Arabia announced judicial reforms said to include restricting the death penalty for drug offenses. In 2020, the Kingdom carried out two drug-related executions. In 2025, that number was 240. Reform rhetoric has not merely failed to produce results. The trajectory has moved in the opposite direction, by orders of magnitude.

Saudi Arabia's **Vision 2030** program prioritizes international image management. As Jeed Basyouni of Reprieve observed: "In Saudi Crown Prince Mohammed bin Salman's Saudi Arabia you can attend a rave in the desert, but you can also be executed for smoking hashish." The contradiction between the Kingdom's brand and its execution statistics is a pressure point that advocates can exploit.

Quiet diplomacy alone does not produce systemic change. Sustained, public, coordinated international pressure, coupled with active consular engagement by sending-country governments, is what the record shows can save individual lives and shift institutional behavior. The 188 foreign nationals executed in 2025 were workers, parents, and individuals whose governments and the international legal system failed to protect them.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can a foreign embassy intervene to stop an execution in Saudi Arabia?

Yes, but success depends on diplomatic intensity. Under VCCR Article 36, embassies have the right to consular notification and access. Saudi Arabia routinely fails to notify. The Sri Lanka case (October 2026) shows that sustained, high-level pressure can result in commutation, though many labor-exporting governments do not prioritize these interventions.

### Is the death penalty for drug offenses legal under international law?

No, under prevailing standards. General Comment No. 36 (2019) restricts "most serious crimes" to those involving intentional killing. The Arab Charter on Human Rights, which Saudi Arabia ratified, mirrors this in Article 6. The 240 drug executions in 2025 are prima facie inconsistent with these obligations.

### How many foreign nationals has Saudi Arabia executed in recent years?

In 2025, 188 out of 356 total (53%). In 2024, 138 out of 345 (40%). The most frequently executed nationalities over the past decade: Pakistanis (155), Syrians (66), Jordanians (50), Yemenis (39), Egyptians (33), and Nigerians (32), per Amnesty International.

### What is the kafala system and how does it affect capital cases?

The kafala system ties a migrant worker's legal status to their employer. Workers cannot change jobs or leave without sponsor permission. Oxford researchers (Hoyle, Harry, Hutton, *Punishment & Society*, 2024) documented how this dependency exposes workers to coercion into drug transport. Some executed as traffickers may more accurately be described as trafficking victims.

### Does Saudi Arabia notify foreign embassies when their nationals face execution?

It is obligated to under VCCR Article 36 but systematically fails to comply. In October 2026, two Bangladeshi nationals were executed in Mecca with no consular notification. The ICJ held in *LaGrand* (2001) and *Avena* (2004) that Article 36 creates individual rights, making these failures violations of binding international law.
