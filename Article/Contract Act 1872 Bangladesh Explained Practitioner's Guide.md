# Contract Act 1872 Bangladesh Explained: A Practitioner's Complete Guide

The Contract Act 1872 spans 238 sections across 11 chapters, yet most Bangladesh-focused legal guides offer little more than textbook summaries. Practitioners are left stitching together scattered High Court Division and Appellate Division decisions, cross-referencing stamping requirements, and calculating limitation periods with no single, consolidated reference point. The gap between the statute on paper and its courtroom application is where cases are won or lost.

This guide treats the **Contract Act 1872 Bangladesh** as working practitioners encounter it. Formation through remedies, each stage maps to its specific statutory provision, annotated with leading Bangladesh court decisions, and flagged with the procedural traps that derail claims. Coverage extends to electronic contracts under the ICT Act 2006, stamp duty pitfalls under the Stamp Act 1899, limitation periods under the Limitation Act 1908, and arbitration under the Arbitration Act 2001.

Whether you are drafting a commercial agreement, advising on a disputed performance, or preparing a brief for breach of contract litigation, the analysis below is structured to function as a desk reference. Every assertion ties back to a section number or a reported decision.

## Essential Elements of a Valid Contract Under the Contract Act 1872

The formation chain under the Act follows a precise sequence. A **proposal** (Section 2(a)) that is accepted (Section 2(b)) creates a **promise**. A promise supported by **consideration** (Section 2(d)) becomes an **agreement** (Section 2(e)). An agreement enforceable by law becomes a **contract** (Section 2(h)).

Breaking this chain at any link means no contract exists. Nine essential elements must satisfy Section 10:

1. **Offer and acceptance** (Sections 3-9). A proposal must be communicated, and acceptance must be absolute and unqualified. Conditional acceptance operates as a counter-offer.
2. **Intention to create legal relations.** Though not expressly stated in the Act, Bangladesh courts infer this requirement. Social and domestic arrangements carry a presumption against such intention.
3. **Lawful consideration** (Sections 2(d), 23-25). Consideration must be real but need not be adequate. Section 25 carves three exceptions where an agreement without consideration remains enforceable: a written promise from natural love and affection between near relations, a promise to compensate for a past voluntary act, and a written promise to pay a time-barred debt.
4. **Capacity to contract** (Section 11). Parties must be of the age of majority (18 years under the Majority Act 1875), of sound mind, and not disqualified by law. A minor's agreement is void ab initio per Mohori Bibee v. Dharmodas Ghose (1903). This voidness is absolute: a minor cannot ratify the agreement upon attaining majority because no agreement exists to ratify.
5. **Free consent** (Sections 13-22). Consent must not result from coercion, undue influence, fraud, misrepresentation, or mistake.
6. **Lawful object** (Section 23). The object must not be forbidden by law, defeat any provision of law, be fraudulent, involve injury to person or property, or be immoral or opposed to public policy.
7. **Writing where required.** The Act itself does not mandate writing for most contracts, but other statutes impose writing requirements for specific categories, such as the Registration Act 1908 for immovable property transfers.
8. **Certainty** (Section 29). Agreements that are uncertain or incapable of being made certain are void.
9. **Not declared void** (Sections 24-30). The agreement must not fall within any category the Act specifically declares void.

**Sound mind** under Section 12 carries a notable procedural implication. A person who is usually of unsound mind but occasionally of sound mind may contract during lucid intervals. The burden of proving unsoundness at the time of contract rests on the party alleging it.

The adequacy of consideration is irrelevant under the Act. Section 25, Explanation 2 expressly states that inadequacy does not render an agreement void, though courts may weigh it when assessing whether consent was freely given.

## Free Consent and Defects That Invalidate Contracts in Bangladesh

Nearly half of all contested contract cases in Bangladesh turn on whether consent was truly free. Section 14 defines **free consent** as consent not caused by coercion, undue influence, fraud, misrepresentation, or mistake. Where any of these five vitiating factors causes consent, the contract is either void or voidable depending on the specific defect.

**Coercion** (Section 15) encompasses committing or threatening to commit any act forbidden by the Penal Code, or unlawfully detaining or threatening to detain any property. Its scope extends beyond common law duress, which traditionally required threats of physical violence. Under the Act, even threats to property or threats to institute criminal proceedings can constitute coercion. A contract induced by coercion is voidable at the option of the coerced party under Section 19.

**Undue influence** (Section 16) arises where one party holds a position to dominate the will of the other and exploits that position for an unfair advantage. In Abul Hossain v. Farooq Sobhan, the court held that an official position creates a presumption of undue influence.

Practitioners handling transactions involving fiduciary relationships, employer-employee contracts, or authority-subordinate dealings should document independent legal advice obtained by the weaker party. This documentation provides the strongest safeguard against subsequent undue influence claims.

**Fraud** (Section 17) requires an intent to deceive. It includes suggestio falsi (active misrepresentation), suppressio veri (active concealment of a material fact by one with a duty to disclose), promises made without intent to perform, and any other deceptive act.

**Misrepresentation** (Section 18) involves innocent false statements of material fact, breach of duty that gains an advantage by misleading, or causing a party to mistake the substance of the agreement. The critical distinction is intent. Fraud renders the contract voidable and entitles the aggrieved party to damages under Section 19. Misrepresentation renders the contract voidable but limits relief to rescission without damages.

**Mistake** operates differently depending on its nature. A bilateral mistake as to a matter of fact essential to the agreement renders the contract void under Section 20. A unilateral mistake of fact generally affords no relief under Section 22. A mistake of law is no excuse under Section 21, consistent with the maxim ignorantia juris non excusat.

For quick reference, the practical matrix:

- Coercion, undue influence, fraud, misrepresentation: contract is **voidable** (aggrieved party may rescind or affirm).
- Bilateral mistake of essential fact: contract is **void** (no contract ever formed).
- Unilateral mistake, mistake of law: **no relief** (contract stands).

## Void and Voidable Agreements: Key Distinctions for Bangladesh Practitioners

Confusing void and voidable agreements ranks among the most common errors in Bangladesh contract litigation. The consequences are severe. A void agreement (Section 2(g)) produces no legal effect from inception, and neither party can enforce it or claim remedies under the contract itself.

A voidable agreement (Section 2(i)) remains valid and enforceable until the aggrieved party elects to avoid it.

The Act declares the following categories of agreements void:

- Agreements without consideration, except the three Section 25 exceptions (Section 25).
- Agreements in restraint of trade (Section 27). Every agreement that restrains a person from exercising a lawful profession, trade, or business is void to that extent.
- Agreements in restraint of legal proceedings (Section 28). The Appellate Division in Bangladesh Air Service v. British Airways, 5BLT AD-242, confirmed that agreements to refer disputes to arbitration do not violate Section 28.
- Agreements in restraint of marriage (Section 26).
- Agreements the meaning of which is uncertain (Section 29).
- Wagering agreements (Section 30).

Agreements that become void occupy a distinct category. Under Section 56, a contract to do an act that becomes impossible or unlawful after formation becomes void at that point. The contract was valid at formation, but subsequent events rendered it void. This differs from an agreement void from inception.

Voidable contracts under Section 2(i) remain binding unless the aggrieved party exercises the right to rescind. The election must occur within a reasonable time. Delay or conduct inconsistent with rescission may constitute affirmation, and once affirmed, the right to rescind is lost.

This time-sensitivity catches practitioners off guard. Advising a client to "wait and see" can extinguish the very remedy you are preserving.

The Appellate Division in M/S. Nur Mohammad v. Bangladesh, 61 DLR (AD) (2009) 77, reinforced that a promisee who elects to treat a contract as subsisting after anticipatory breach cannot later claim the contract was void.

Regarding restitution, even where an agreement is void, Sections 65 and 66 require restoration of benefits received. Any person who received an advantage under a void agreement must restore it or make compensation, preventing unjust enrichment despite the absence of an enforceable contract.

## Types of Breach of Contract and Their Legal Consequences

What distinguishes a minor contractual failure from one that entitles the aggrieved party to walk away from the entire agreement? The answer determines whether your client can only claim damages for a shortfall or can treat the entire contract as discharged.

**Breach of contract** occurs when a party fails to perform an obligation without lawful excuse. Bangladesh law recognizes four categories, though the Act itself does not use these exact labels.

A **minor breach** (partial breach) involves failure to perform a non-essential term. The aggrieved party may claim damages but cannot treat the contract as discharged. Both parties must continue performing their respective obligations.

A **material breach** involves failure to perform a term that goes to the root of the contract. This entitles the aggrieved party to treat the contract as discharged and claim damages. Whether a breach is material depends on the importance of the breached term relative to the contract as a whole.

A **fundamental breach** deprives the innocent party of substantially the whole benefit of the contract. In Ibrahim (Md.) v. Government of Bangladesh, 2012, 41 CLC (HCD), non-delivery of contracted goods constituted a fundamental breach justifying discharge.

**Anticipatory breach** receives express statutory treatment under Section 39. When a party signifies an intention not to perform, or makes performance impossible, the promisee may end the contract immediately. The promisee need not wait until the date of performance to sue.

Sonali Bank v. Kamaphuli Works Ltd, 2BLT AD-78, confirmed that damages for anticipatory breach are assessed by reference to definite sums where the contractual obligation is for a fixed amount.

Practitioners must distinguish breach from frustration under Section 56. Breach involves fault; frustration involves none. This distinction determines whether the non-performing party faces a damages claim or both parties receive discharge.

When responding to a breach, document it contemporaneously. Issue a written notice specifying the breach, the contractual provision violated, and the remedy sought. If the breach is material or fundamental, communicate the election to terminate clearly.

Continued performance after knowledge of the breach may constitute affirmation, barring subsequent termination. The notice creates the evidentiary record courts rely on when assessing whether the innocent party acted reasonably.

## Remedies for Breach of Contract: Damages, Specific Performance, and Injunctions

Knowing which remedy to pursue, and in what sequence, separates effective advocacy from wasted litigation costs. The default remedy for breach of contract is damages. Specific performance and injunctions are equitable remedies available only when damages prove inadequate.

**Damages under Section 73** compensate for loss or damage caused by the breach that naturally arose in the usual course of things, or which the parties knew at the time of contracting to be likely to result from the breach. Remote or speculative losses are excluded. The injured party need only prove the causal connection between breach and loss.

**Section 74** governs liquidated damages and penalties. Where parties have agreed upon a sum payable in case of breach, the court may award reasonable compensation not exceeding the stipulated amount. Bangladesh courts do not enforce penalty clauses at face value. They assess whether the stipulated amount represents a genuine pre-estimate of loss or an unconscionable penalty, and award what is reasonable.

**Specific performance** under Section 12 of the Specific Relief Act 1877 (as applicable in Bangladesh) is discretionary. Courts grant it where monetary compensation would be inadequate, typically for contracts involving immovable property or unique goods. Courts refuse it for personal service contracts, contracts requiring continuous supervision, and cases where the plaintiff acted inequitably.

Mohammad Ali Miah v. Md Sekander Ali, 15 BLT 484, illustrates the strict temporal limits on seeking this remedy (discussed further under limitation periods below).

**Quantum meruit** allows recovery for the value of work done or services rendered when a contract is discharged before full performance, or when the other party prevents completion. This restitutionary claim prevents unjust enrichment.

**Injunctions** under Section 54 of the Specific Relief Act 1877 restrain a party from committing a breach. Prof MA Raquib v. Prof Zillur Rahman, 37 DLR 83, established key principles governing permanent injunctions. Temporary injunctions under Order 39, Rules 1 and 2 of the Code of Civil Procedure 1908 require demonstration of a prima facie case, balance of convenience, and irreparable harm.

The practitioner's decision tree: pursue damages first as the default. If damages are inadequate because the subject matter is unique or monetary calculation is impossible, seek specific performance. If the breach involves a negative covenant or threatens irreparable harm, seek an injunction.

The duty to mitigate applies throughout. The injured party must take reasonable steps to minimize loss. Failure to mitigate reduces recoverable damages by the amount reasonable action could have avoided.

## Frustration of Contract Under Section 56: Impossibility and Force Majeure

The COVID-19 pandemic generated more Section 56 litigation in Bangladesh than any single event in the Act's history. Most claims failed, and understanding why requires a precise reading of the provision and the judicial tests Bangladesh courts apply.

Section 56 provides that a contract to do an act which, after the contract is made, becomes impossible or unlawful by reason of some event the promisor could not prevent, becomes void. The contract discharges automatically. Neither party bears fault, and neither party can claim damages for non-performance.

The impossibility test is absolute, not relative. Commercial hardship, increased expense, or reduced profitability does not qualify. Pubali Bank Ltd v. Sultana Oil Mills, 19 BLD (HCD) 249, established that mere difficulty or financial inconvenience does not amount to impossibility under Section 56.

The promisor must show that performance has become literally or legally impossible, not merely more burdensome.

Section 56 applies to leases. In Mokbul Hossain v. Mosammat Jaheda Khatoon, 14 BLD (HCD) 549, the High Court Division held that Section 56 can render a lease void where the subject matter is destroyed or the lease's purpose becomes impossible to fulfill.

**Force majeure** is contractual, not statutory, and appears nowhere in the Contract Act 1872. A force majeure clause allocates risk for specified extraordinary events through negotiated contractual terms. Unlike Section 56, which voids the contract entirely, a force majeure clause typically suspends performance without voiding the agreement.

Where a contract contains no force majeure clause, a party seeking relief from non-performance must rely on Section 56 and its demanding impossibility threshold.

Most COVID-era lease frustration claims failed because physical performance of lease obligations remained possible even under movement restrictions. The lessee could still occupy or access the property, and the lessor's obligation to provide quiet enjoyment was not rendered impossible.

Drafting tip: define force majeure events with specificity. Generic references to "acts of God" invite litigation. List pandemic, epidemic, government-imposed lockdown, import or export restriction, and supply chain disruption as named events. Specify consequences clearly: suspension of obligations, notice requirements, duration thresholds triggering termination rights, and allocation of costs during the force majeure period.

## Electronic Contracts and Digital Signatures Under Bangladesh Law

The ICT Act 2006 transformed the enforceability of electronic agreements in Bangladesh, yet many practitioners still treat e-contracts with unnecessary caution. Section 5 of the ICT Act 2006 grants legal recognition to information in electronic form. Section 6 validates the use of electronic records where the law requires information to be in writing.

**Electronic contracts** are valid and enforceable in Bangladesh. Offer and acceptance communicated through electronic means satisfy the formation requirements of the Contract Act 1872, provided the essential elements under Section 10 are otherwise met. Sections 2(ia) and 2(ic) of the ICT Act 2006 define **digital signatures** and **electronic signatures** respectively. Seven licensed Certifying Authorities operate in Bangladesh, authorized to issue digital signature certificates.

The **Evidence (Amendment) Act 2022** strengthened the evidentiary framework for electronic contracts. A presumption now favors the authenticity of documents bearing secure digital signatures. The burden of disproving authenticity shifts to the party challenging the digital signature. This procedural advantage rewards parties who invest in proper digital signing infrastructure.

Several categories of instruments remain excluded from electronic execution. Powers of attorney for immovable property transactions, sale deeds for immovable property, wills, mortgage deeds, and marriage contracts still require physical signatures. Practitioners must verify that their specific transaction type qualifies for electronic execution before relying on digital signatures.

The most significant practitioner trap sits at the intersection of electronic contracts and stamping. The **Stamp Act 1899** predates electronic commerce by a century, but its requirements remain fully applicable. E-contracts carry the same stamp duty obligations as physical contracts.

An electronically signed agreement that lacks proper stamping is inadmissible in evidence under Section 35 of the Stamp Act 1899. The conservative practice: print the electronic contract, affix the required stamp papers, and maintain both electronic and physical versions. This dual-format approach provides maximum evidentiary protection.

Practitioners should maintain complete digital audit trails for all electronic contracts. Use licensed Certifying Authorities for high-value transactions. Preserve electronic evidence in compliance with the Evidence Act, including server logs, email chains establishing offer and acceptance, and metadata confirming the identity of signatories and the time of execution.

## Stamp Duty, Limitation Periods, and Enforceability Essentials

Stamp duty deficiency and limitation expiry defeat more contract claims in Bangladesh than any substantive legal argument. Both are threshold issues: a court will not reach the merits if the contract is unstamped or the claim is time-barred.

**Stamp Act 1899, Section 35** renders unstamped or insufficiently stamped instruments inadmissible in evidence. This exclusion is mandatory. A court presented with an unstamped contract must refuse to admit it, regardless of the underlying claim's merits.

The cure exists but punishes: pay the deficient stamp duty plus a penalty of up to 10 times the deficiency amount, subject to judicial permission. Even with the cure, rectification delays and costs can be significant.

Verify stamping at the earliest stage. Before accepting instructions on a contract dispute, examine the original instrument. If stamp duty is deficient, advise the client immediately. The cost of retrospective stamping with penalties is a material factor in assessing claim viability.

The **Limitation Act 1908** imposes strict time bars. For specific performance suits, Article 113 prescribes three years from the date fixed for performance. For general contract suits, Article 120 prescribes six years. Courts enforce these periods strictly.

Mohammad Ali Miah v. Md Sekander Ali, 15 BLT 484, demonstrates their severity. A specific performance claim filed at four years and two months was held time-barred. The court rejected the argument that subsequent refusals by the defendant should restart the limitation clock. Limitation begins when the right to sue first accrues, and subsequent refusals do not generate a fresh cause of action.

Section 14 of the Limitation Act 1908 excludes time spent prosecuting proceedings in a court that lacked jurisdiction, provided the prosecution was in good faith. This exclusion can rescue an otherwise time-barred claim, but only where the initial wrong-court filing was genuinely bona fide.

A critical interaction trap: time spent curing a stamp deficiency does not toll limitation. If you discover a stamping deficiency while limitation runs, you face two clocks simultaneously. File suit to preserve limitation, and address the stamping deficiency concurrently.

**Practitioner checklist:**
- Verify stamping before accepting instructions.
- Calculate the limitation period at initial consultation.
- If limitation approaches, file a protective suit even if settlement negotiations continue.
- Never assume that subsequent demands or refusals restart the limitation clock.

## Arbitration and Alternative Dispute Resolution for Contract Disputes

The **Arbitration Act 2001**, modeled on the UNCITRAL Model Law, provides a comprehensive framework for resolving contract disputes outside the courts. For high-value commercial contracts, arbitration offers confidentiality, party autonomy in selecting arbitrators, and international enforceability of awards.

Section 9 requires the arbitration agreement to be in writing. This includes an arbitration clause in a contract or a separate agreement, provided it appears in a signed document, an exchange of letters, or an exchange of statements of claim and defense where one party alleges an agreement and the other does not deny it.

Section 28 of the Contract Act 1872 renders void agreements that restrain parties from enforcing rights through ordinary legal proceedings. The Appellate Division in Bangladesh Air Service v. British Airways, 5BLT AD-242, held that arbitration clauses do not violate Section 28. Arbitration agreements, including those specifying foreign seats, are valid and enforceable.

Bangladesh, as a signatory to the New York Convention, enforces foreign arbitral awards subject to limited refusal grounds: incapacity, invalidity of the agreement, lack of notice, excess of jurisdiction, improper tribunal composition, or public policy.

Southern Solar Power v. BPDB (2019) clarified that Bangladesh courts retain jurisdiction to grant interim relief even where the underlying dispute falls under an arbitration clause. This matters for practitioners seeking urgent injunctive relief or preservation orders.

**Drafting tips for arbitration clauses:** Specify the seat of arbitration, which determines the governing procedural law. State the governing law of the contract. Fix the number of arbitrators (one or three). Name the appointing authority: Bangladesh International Arbitration Centre (BIAC), Singapore International Arbitration Centre (SIAC), or International Chamber of Commerce (ICC). Specify the language of proceedings.

Multi-tier dispute resolution clauses are increasingly common in major commercial contracts, typically requiring negotiation, then mediation, then arbitration if mediation fails. Bangladesh courts have shown increasing receptivity to mediation, and the Money Loan Court Act 2003 mandates ADR attempts in financial disputes.

## Frequently Asked Questions About the Contract Act 1872 in Bangladesh

### What is the difference between void and voidable agreements under Bangladesh law?

A void agreement (Section 2(g)) produces no legal effect from inception; neither party can enforce it. A voidable agreement (Section 2(i)) remains valid until the aggrieved party rescinds it. The practical consequence: a voidable contract can be affirmed through delay or conduct, becoming fully enforceable. Void agreements cannot be affirmed because no valid agreement ever existed.

### When will a Bangladesh court grant specific performance rather than damages?

Courts grant specific performance under Section 12 of the Specific Relief Act 1877 when monetary compensation would be inadequate, typically for immovable property or unique goods with no available substitute. Courts refuse it for personal service contracts, contracts requiring continuous supervision, and cases involving inequitable plaintiff conduct. The remedy is discretionary.

### How long do I have to sue for breach of contract in Bangladesh?

Three years for specific performance (Article 113, Limitation Act 1908) from the date fixed for performance. Six years for general contract suits (Article 120). As Mohammad Ali Miah v. Md Sekander Ali, 15 BLT 484 confirmed, filing beyond the three-year window for specific performance is fatal. Subsequent demands or refusals do not restart the clock.

### Does Section 56 frustration apply to COVID-related non-performance?

In most cases, no. Section 56 requires absolute impossibility, not commercial difficulty. Pubali Bank Ltd v. Sultana Oil Mills, 19 BLD (HCD) 249 set this threshold. COVID restrictions generally made performance harder or costlier, not impossible. Parties with contractual force majeure clauses fared better than those relying on Section 56 alone.

### Is arbitration mandatory if a contract has an arbitration clause in Bangladesh?

Yes. Under the Arbitration Act 2001, a court must refer parties to arbitration if a valid agreement exists and one party applies before submitting a defense on the merits. Bangladesh Air Service v. British Airways, 5BLT AD-242 confirmed enforceability, including for foreign-seated arbitrations. Courts retain interim relief jurisdiction per Southern Solar Power v. BPDB (2019).

### What happens if a contract lacks proper stamp duty in Bangladesh?

The contract becomes inadmissible in evidence under Section 35 of the Stamp Act 1899. The court cannot consider the document until the deficiency is cured by paying the shortfall plus a penalty of up to 10 times the deficiency, with judicial permission. Time spent curing the deficiency does not pause limitation, creating a risk that the claim expires during rectification.
