# Can Sheikh Hasina Return to Bangladesh After the ICT Death Penalty Verdict?

"They may arrest me on my return, they may even kill me. Still, I have to go." Sheikh Hasina's words to Reuters in November 2026 carry the weight of a former prime minister confronting a death sentence handed down by a tribunal her own government created. The **International Crimes Tribunal (ICT)** convicted her in absentia on November 17, 2025, sentencing her to death for crimes against humanity. The 30-day statutory appeal window has expired. The sentence is legally final.

Hasina's Awami League government established the ICT in 2010 under the International Crimes (Tribunals) Act 1973 to prosecute atrocities committed during the 1971 Liberation War. That same tribunal is now the instrument of her conviction, this time for a crackdown on student protesters in July and August 2024 that the UN Office of the High Commissioner for Human Rights estimates killed approximately 1,400 people.

Can Sheikh Hasina return to Bangladesh after the ICT death penalty? I examine the legal status of the verdict, the consequences of voluntary return, and whether extradition can compel her surrender.

## The ICT Verdict: Charges, Conviction, and Current Legal Status

The tribunal convicted Hasina on three of five charges. Even the ICT itself rejected two counts brought by the prosecution, yet it imposed the maximum sentence: death. Co-defendant Asaduzzaman Khan Kamal, the former Home Minister, received the same sentence. The case, ICT Case No. 02 of 2025, was heard by a three-judge bench.

The charges centered on crimes against humanity arising from the government's violent suppression of student-led protests between July and August 2024. The UN OHCHR estimated approximately 1,400 deaths during the crackdown. The tribunal found Hasina bore command responsibility for the security forces' actions on three of the five counts.

Under Section 21 of the ICT Act 1973, any party to the proceedings has 30 days from the date of the order to file an appeal. That window has closed. ICT Chief Prosecutor Mohammad Aminul Islam confirmed the finality: "Whether it is Sheikh Hasina's or Abul Kalam Azad's, any fugitive accused or anyone in prison, the appeal must be filed within 30 days."

The death sentence is not Hasina's only legal liability. She faces over 100 additional cases in Bangladeshi courts. On November 27, 2025, she received a 21-year sentence for corruption. In July 2025, the ICT imposed a six-month contempt sentence. The UN OHCHR acknowledged the verdict as an "important moment for victims" while expressing regret over the imposition of the death penalty. Amnesty International Secretary General Agnes Callamard called it a "grave miscarriage of justice" and described the proceedings as "deeply flawed."

## What Happens If Hasina Voluntarily Returns to Bangladesh

There is no statutory mechanism for a "safe return" or conditional surrender under the ICT Act 1973. The law offers a binary outcome: apprehension and enforcement of the sentence.

An active arrest warrant remains outstanding. Bangladesh's Law Minister has stated publicly that the death sentence is enforceable upon apprehension "even before she can surrender." Any entry at any border checkpoint would trigger immediate arrest. The ICT Act contains no provision for a grace period, negotiated terms, or conditional presentation before the tribunal.

Hasina herself appears to understand this. Her November 2026 Reuters interview acknowledges both arrest and death as probable outcomes, yet she insists on return. Whether this reflects political calculation or genuine resolve, the legal reality is unyielding.

The concurrent cases compound her jeopardy. Even in a hypothetical scenario where the ICT death sentence were somehow stayed, the 21-year corruption conviction and the six-month contempt sentence provide independent legal grounds for detention. The Bangladesh Passport Order 1973, Sections 6 and 7, has already revoked her travel documents, further constraining any planned return.

The diplomatic dimension also matters. Hasina currently resides in India. Any voluntary return would require transit arrangements that implicate India's foreign policy calculations, particularly given the pending extradition request. India has not publicly indicated whether it would permit or facilitate her departure.

## The Appeal Window: Is Any Legal Avenue Still Open?

If the tribunal itself was unconstitutionally composed, can the sentence it imposed survive judicial review? This is the question Hasina's legal team would need to press, but the procedural obstacles are severe.

The 30-day appeal window under Section 21 of the ICT Act contains no extension mechanism. The statute is silent on late filings, and no court has recognized an inherent power to enlarge time under the ICT Act. That direct appellate route is closed.

A constitutional challenge, however, may remain theoretically available. Four amendments to the ICT Act were enacted by ordinance without subsequent parliamentary validation. Under Article 93 of the Bangladesh Constitution, ordinances that are not validated by Parliament within 30 days of the next session cease to have effect. Article 7(2) declares any law inconsistent with the Constitution to be void. If these amendments are constitutionally void, the proceedings conducted under their authority may be challengeable.

There is also the question of bench composition. The ICT bench consisted of Additional Judges, who are probationary appointees, rather than permanent High Court judges. Articles 22 and 94(4) of the Constitution establish the independence and qualifications of the judiciary. If probationary judges lacked constitutional authority to sit on the tribunal, this presents a structural defect going to jurisdiction.

The practical obstacle is formidable. Any constitutional challenge requires access to Bangladeshi courts. That means either physical return (and immediate arrest) or filing through proxy counsel in an environment that the International Bar Association's Human Rights Institute has described as one of harassment and intimidation of defence lawyers. Arafat Khan, writing in Opinio Juris in 2026, characterized the broader dynamic as "autocratic legalism," where legitimate institutions are weaponized to foreclose the very legal remedies they nominally provide.

## The Extradition Question: The 2013 India Treaty and Grounds for Refusal

India has at least three independent legal grounds to refuse extradition, each sufficient on its own.

Bangladesh submitted a formal note verbale to India in November 2025 requesting Hasina's extradition. As of July 2026, India's official position is that it is "examining" the request. The applicable framework is the India-Bangladesh Extradition Treaty of 2013, supplemented by India's domestic Extradition Act.

The first ground is the "unjust or oppressive" exception. Article 8 of the 2013 Treaty permits the requested state to refuse extradition where surrender would be unjust or oppressive having regard to all the circumstances. The fair trial deficits documented by the IBAHRI, Amnesty International, and multiple academic commentators provide substantial factual basis for invoking this provision.

The second ground is the *Soering* principle. In *Soering v. United Kingdom* (ECtHR, App. No. 14038/88, 1989), the European Court of Human Rights held that extradition to face the death penalty, particularly following proceedings that do not meet fair trial standards, may violate the prohibition on inhuman or degrading treatment. While India is not bound by the ECHR, the *Soering* principle has been widely adopted as a norm of customary international law. The Oxford Law Faculty's Death Penalty Research Unit confirmed in December 2025 that the death sentence "strengthens the asylum claim."

The third ground is India's domestic commutation condition. Section 34C of India's Extradition Act permits India to impose a condition that the death penalty will not be carried out. The ICT Act 1973, however, contains no mechanism for Bangladesh to accept or give effect to such a condition. India can demand commutation, but Bangladesh cannot legally deliver it.

## The In Absentia Problem: Fair Trial Deficits as Extradition Obstacles

None of the four internationally recognized safeguards for in absentia trials were satisfied in Hasina's case. This is not my assessment alone. It is the conclusion of the Opinio Juris symposium convened in March 2026, the IBAHRI, and Human Rights Watch.

The four safeguards, derived from Article 14 of the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights and the jurisprudence of international criminal tribunals, require: (1) actual notice of the proceedings to the accused, (2) a genuine and voluntary waiver of the right to be present, (3) representation by counsel of the accused's own choosing, and (4) the right to a retrial or de novo review upon apprehension. The Opinio Juris symposium identified the absence of retrial provisions as "the core condition" missing and urged Bangladesh to offer "diplomatic assurance of retrial" as a precondition for extradition cooperation.

The evidentiary record compounds the problem. Leaked audio recordings from the National Telecommunication Monitoring Centre were admitted into evidence without voir dire proceedings to establish their authenticity, chain of custody, or the legality of their collection. This raises concerns under Article 43 of the Bangladesh Constitution and Article 17 of the ICCPR, both of which protect against arbitrary interference with privacy.

Cross-examination of prosecution witnesses was prohibited by the tribunal. This directly violates Article 14(3)(e) of the ICCPR, which guarantees the right of the accused to examine witnesses against them. Meenakshi Ganguly of Human Rights Watch stated: "No one should be extradited if they would face a trial abroad that does not meet international fair trial standards and could result in the death penalty."

The IBAHRI found that the harassment and intimidation of defence lawyers "strike at the heart of the right to a fair trial." UK courts, in earlier cases involving the ICT, noted "widespread international criticism" of the tribunal for failing to meet minimum fair trial standards.

## The Abul Kalam Azad Precedent: Lessons for Hasina's Case

One ICT death convict has already returned to Bangladesh. His case reveals both a possible pathway and its fragility.

Abul Kalam Azad was sentenced to death by the ICT in 2013 for crimes committed during the 1971 Liberation War. He fled Bangladesh and remained a fugitive for 13 years. On October 22, 2025, the government suspended his sentence under Section 401 of the Code of Criminal Procedure on the condition that he surrender and pursue an appeal. He surrendered to the ICT in January 2026.

The ICT Chief Prosecutor promptly declared the arrangement "illegal and unlawful," creating a live legal dispute over whether executive clemency under Section 401 CrPC can override a death sentence imposed by the ICT. The Supreme Court's Appellate Division has adjourned the question without resolution.

The distinction between Azad's case and Hasina's is political, not legal. Azad was convicted of 1971 war crimes, a category of offence that carries a different political valence under the current government. Hasina's conviction arises from the acts of her own administration. The current government has no discernible political incentive to offer her the Section 401 route.

If the ICT Prosecutor's position prevails, and the Section 401 arrangement is deemed illegal, it closes the only demonstrated pathway for a negotiated return of an ICT death convict. The precedent is simultaneously instructive and cautionary.

## The Death Sentence Paradox: How Severity Shields Against Accountability

Did Bangladesh's decision to seek the death penalty ensure that it will never be carried out? The Oxford Law Faculty's Death Penalty Research Unit posed precisely this question in December 2025, concluding that the death sentence is the "fastest way to strengthen asylum claim of fugitive ruler."

The paradox operates through four interlocking mechanisms. The death penalty triggers non-refoulement protections under international law, blocking extradition. The documented fair trial deficits give third states independent grounds to refuse cooperation. The absence of retrial provisions removes the standard cure for in absentia convictions. And the severity of the sentence makes voluntary return irrational absent guarantees the Bangladeshi state cannot legally provide.

The result is an accountability gap. The families of the estimated 1,400 people killed during the 2024 crackdown have a legitimate claim to justice. The ICT verdict was, in the words of the UN OHCHR spokesperson Ravina Shamdasani, an "important moment for victims." Yet the procedural choices made by the tribunal and the prosecution have constructed a legal fortress around the very person convicted.

## How Sheikh Hasina Could Return and Avoid Execution: Every Legal Pathway Available

The legal landscape I have analyzed above is severe but not without exits. Seven realistic pathways exist for Hasina to return to Bangladesh without facing execution. Each carries distinct risks and none is guaranteed. But a competent legal strategy would pursue several simultaneously, layering domestic remedies with international mechanisms and diplomatic leverage. Here is the complete roadmap.

### 1. Conditional Surrender Under Section 401 CrPC (the Azad Route)

The most direct route follows a precedent already tested. **Section 401 of the Code of Criminal Procedure** empowers the government to suspend any death sentence at any time, including those imposed by the ICT. This is a general executive power of broad application, and nothing in the ICT Act 1973 explicitly overrides it.

The precedent is concrete. Abul Kalam Azad received an ICT death sentence in 2013 for crimes against humanity committed during the 1971 Liberation War. He fled Bangladesh and remained a fugitive for 13 years. On October 22, 2025, the government suspended his sentence under Section 401 CrPC on condition he surrender and file an appeal. He surrendered in January 2026. The Appellate Division is now considering whether to admit the belated appeal.

The ICT Chief Prosecutor declared the arrangement "illegal," but the Supreme Court has not ruled definitively on whether Section 401 CrPC overrides Section 21's 30-day appeal limit. The legal argument available to Hasina is straightforward: Section 401 is a general executive power, and the ICT Act contains no express exclusion of its application.

If the appeal is admitted, the appellate grounds are substantial: void ordinance amendments under Article 93 of the Constitution, improper bench composition under Articles 22 and 94(4), inadmissible NTMC recordings violating Article 43 of the Constitution and Article 17 of the ICCPR, and denial of cross-examination rights guaranteed by Article 14(3)(e) of the ICCPR. None of these arguments is frivolous. Each goes to jurisdiction and due process.

The most powerful supporting precedent is *ATM Azharul Islam v. The State* (Appellate Division, Supreme Court of Bangladesh, May 27, 2025). A seven-justice full bench acquitted him and ordered immediate release. This was the first ever overturning of an ICT death sentence by the Appellate Division. It establishes that ICT death sentences are not immune from full appellate review.

### 2. Diplomatic Assurance of Retrial (India's Key Negotiating Condition)

India has publicly stated in November 2026 that it has placed conditions on Hasina's return: a Bangladeshi military aircraft must come to India to transport her safely, and she must be guaranteed the right to appeal her sentence and face a fair trial.

The **Opinio Juris symposium** of March 2026 on Hasina's case specifically recommended that Bangladesh offer "diplomatic assurance of retrial" as the standard international law cure for in absentia convictions lacking fair-trial safeguards. This is an established mechanism. A state resolves fair trial concerns by guaranteeing that upon return, the accused receives a de novo hearing with full participatory rights.

The legal basis is Article 14 of the ICCPR. Bangladesh has binding obligations to guarantee fair trial to any accused person within its jurisdiction. A diplomatic assurance of de novo proceedings resolves the in absentia deficit under international law. The Council of Europe's comparative study on in absentia proceedings found that most legal systems require a full retrial, not merely sentence review, where the accused was absent through no fault. This normative body of comparative law supports Hasina's entitlement to a fresh hearing upon surrender.

If Bangladesh formally accepts India's conditions, it creates the legal space for a belated appeal before the Appellate Division, triggering the *ATM Azharul Islam* precedent for full appellate review.

### 3. ICCPR Optional Protocol: UN Human Rights Committee Interim Measures

Bangladesh is a party to the ICCPR and its **First Optional Protocol**, which allows individuals to petition the UN Human Rights Committee after domestic remedies are exhausted or demonstrably unavailable. The Committee can issue **Rule 94 interim measures** requesting Bangladesh not carry out the execution while the communication is under review.

Since 1987 the Committee has examined capital cases under the Optional Protocol. States parties comply in the overwhelming majority of cases. While not legally binding, non-compliance triggers findings of an additional ICCPR violation and significant diplomatic consequences.

The substantive argument is powerful. The UN Human Rights Committee's **General Comment 36** (2018) clarified that a death penalty imposed following a trial that violates Article 14 constitutes an arbitrary deprivation of life under Article 6 of the ICCPR. The in absentia conviction with no effective legal representation is specifically flagged as a potential Article 6 violation. The Committee has found violations in similar cases involving Jamaica and Trinidad.

Hasina's counsel has already engaged the UN Special Rapporteur on extrajudicial, summary or arbitrary executions. This can proceed entirely from India without requiring physical return.

### 4. India's Legal Shield: Article 21, Section 34C, and Formal Asylum

**Article 21 of the Indian Constitution** protects the right to life and personal liberty of all persons, including non-citizens. Indian courts have held that the non-refoulement principle is encompassed within Article 21. Hasina can file a writ petition in the Delhi High Court or Supreme Court of India invoking Article 21 to block any extradition order. Indian courts have protected Rohingya and Sri Lankan Tamil refugees under this doctrine.

**Section 34C of India's Extradition Act 1962** allows India to condition extradition on commutation of the death sentence to life imprisonment. The *Abu Salem* precedent (Supreme Court of India, July 11, 2022) is directly applicable. Abu Salem was extradited from Portugal to India only after India gave a formal sovereign assurance of no death penalty. The Indian Supreme Court enforced this assurance, binding Indian courts from imposing death. India can demand the identical assurance from Bangladesh before any extradition proceeds.

India has no domestic refugee law but grants asylum through executive discretion. The government can formally declare Hasina a protected person, as it did for the Dalai Lama in 1959. This converts the extradition question from a bilateral negotiating issue into a legal protection that forecloses extradition entirely.

### 5. The Öcalan-to-Commutation Pathway: International Pressure Model

In *Öcalan v. Turkey (No. 1)* (ECtHR Grand Chamber, App. No. 46221/99, May 12, 2005), the Court held that imposing a death sentence following an unfair trial before a court whose independence and impartiality were in doubt amounted to inhuman treatment violating Article 3 of the ECHR. Turkey subsequently commuted Öcalan's death sentence to life imprisonment under ECtHR pressure, following Turkey's abolition of capital punishment.

The analogy for Hasina is structural. Her in absentia conviction before a tribunal widely criticised for independence deficits mirrors the problems in Öcalan. Though India is not an ECHR party and the ECtHR has no jurisdiction, the ICCPR Optional Protocol communication pathway produces the same functional result: international human rights body pressure toward commutation.

A finding by the UN Human Rights Committee that the death sentence violates Articles 6 and 14 of the ICCPR would create the normative equivalent of the Öcalan pressure on Turkey. Bangladesh would face strong international incentives to commute.

### 6. Presidential Clemency Under Article 49: The Final Safety Valve

**Article 49 of the Bangladesh Constitution** grants the President power to pardon, reprieve, remit, or commute any sentence passed by any court or tribunal, including the ICT. The *Sarwar Kamal* precedent established that fugitives must surrender before petitioning for presidential clemency, meaning this avenue requires physical return.

The practical pathway unfolds in stages. Hasina returns under a Section 401 CrPC arrangement, files an appeal, builds the international law record of fair-trial deficits, and if the appeal fails, petitions the President under Article 49 citing the OHCHR, Amnesty International, and IBAHRI documentation of procedural unfairness. Thirty death-row convicts have previously received presidential clemency in Bangladesh. The precedent exists.

### 7. The Negotiated Settlement Model: Precedents from Abu Salem, Taylor, and Saleh

International practice offers three models for negotiated returns of fugitive leaders facing capital sentences.

The *Abu Salem* model (India-Portugal, 2002-2022): Portugal extradited Abu Salem to India only upon India's formal sovereign assurance of no death penalty. The Indian Supreme Court enforced this assurance in 2022. Bangladesh can be required to give the same assurance to India as a condition of any transfer.

The *Charles Taylor* model (Nigeria-SCSL, 2003-2006): Taylor went into exile in Nigeria under AU/ECOWAS diplomatic brokerage, using it as shelter while negotiations proceeded. Nigeria facilitated his eventual transfer to the Special Court for Sierra Leone. India has significantly more geopolitical weight than Nigeria. Its protective capacity is correspondingly greater.

The **Yemen/Saleh GCC Immunity Deal** (2011): President Saleh transferred power in exchange for full immunity brokered by Saudi Arabia, the GCC, and endorsed by the UN Security Council. For Hasina, a comparable framework could involve India and Bangladesh negotiating with OIC or UN facilitation. Hasina renounces political activity, returns, and receives a guaranteed commuted sentence and fair trial.

### Where Probability Meets Justice

Of these seven pathways, the highest probability routes are the Section 401 CrPC conditional surrender (already tested in the Azad case), India's diplomatic conditions (already publicly stated), and the ICCPR Optional Protocol communication (already initiated through the Special Rapporteur). A competent legal strategy layers all three simultaneously, with India's Article 21 protection as the safety net and presidential clemency as the long-term fallback.

Justice requires honesty about competing claims. The families of approximately 1,400 people killed during the 2024 crackdown have an undeniable right to accountability. Sheikh Hasina has an undeniable right to a fair trial. These are not contradictory demands. Every pathway I have outlined leads to the same destination: a proceeding where Hasina is present, represented by counsel of her choosing, able to confront the evidence against her, and judged by an independent tribunal. That is not leniency. That is the minimum the law requires before it takes a life.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: Has Sheikh Hasina been sentenced to death by the ICT?

Yes. On November 17, 2025, the International Crimes Tribunal convicted Sheikh Hasina in ICT Case No. 02 of 2025 on three of five charges of crimes against humanity related to the July-August 2024 crackdown. The three-judge bench imposed the death sentence. Co-defendant Asaduzzaman Khan Kamal received the same sentence.

### Q: Can Sheikh Hasina appeal the ICT death sentence?

The 30-day appeal window under Section 21 of the ICT Act 1973 has expired with no extension mechanism available. A constitutional challenge to the tribunal's composition remains theoretically possible but would require access to Bangladeshi courts, effectively demanding either physical return or proxy counsel in a hostile legal environment.

### Q: Will India extradite Sheikh Hasina to Bangladesh?

Extradition is unlikely. India has multiple grounds for refusal under the 2013 India-Bangladesh Extradition Treaty, including Article 8 (unjust or oppressive circumstances), the *Soering* non-refoulement principle, and Section 34C of India's Extradition Act (commutation condition). As of July 2026, India stated it is "examining" the request.

### Q: Has Interpol issued a Red Notice for Sheikh Hasina?

No. Bangladesh requested a Red Notice in early 2025. As of June 2026, Interpol had not issued it and had requested additional documentation. A Red Notice is not an arrest warrant but a request to locate and provisionally detain a person pending extradition.

### Q: What is the Abul Kalam Azad precedent?

Abul Kalam Azad, an ICT death convict for 1971 war crimes, was a fugitive for 13 years before the government suspended his sentence under Section 401 CrPC on condition of surrender and appeal. He surrendered in January 2026. The ICT Chief Prosecutor declared the arrangement "illegal," and the Supreme Court has not resolved the dispute.

### Q: Were international fair trial standards met in Hasina's ICT trial?

No. None of the four recognized safeguards for in absentia trials were met: actual notice, genuine waiver, chosen counsel, and retrial rights. The IBAHRI, Amnesty International, Human Rights Watch, and the UN OHCHR have all identified significant fair trial deficits.

### Q: Does the death sentence make extradition harder?

Yes. The death sentence triggers non-refoulement protections and strengthens asylum claims. The *Soering v. United Kingdom* principle (ECtHR, 1989) establishes that extradition to face the death penalty after proceedings with fair trial deficits may violate the prohibition on inhuman treatment.

### Q: What other cases does Sheikh Hasina face in Bangladesh?

Hasina faces over 100 cases beyond the ICT conviction, including a 21-year corruption sentence (November 27, 2025) and a six-month ICT contempt sentence (July 2025). Each provides independent legal grounds for detention upon return.
