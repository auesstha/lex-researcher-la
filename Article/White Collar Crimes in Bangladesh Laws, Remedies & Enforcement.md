# White Collar Crimes in Bangladesh: Statutory Framework, Remedies, and Enforcement Realities

Global Financial Integrity estimates that approximately $150 billion was illicitly transferred out of Bangladesh during the Hasina era, with annual outflows reaching $12 billion. In 2024, the Anti-Corruption Commission recorded a conviction rate of 48.17%, an eight-year low, while 3,410 cases remained pending across special tribunals. White collar crimes in Bangladesh expose a stark contradiction: the country possesses robust statutes for prosecution, yet its enforcement machinery consistently fails to deliver accountability.

I use the term white collar crime in its Bangladeshi statutory context to mean economically motivated, non-violent offences committed by individuals or corporations occupying positions of trust. The concept originates in Edwin Sutherland's 1939 criminological framework, but I anchor this analysis to five operative statutes: the **Anti-Corruption Commission Act 2004**, the **Money Laundering Prevention Act 2012** (MLPA), the **Securities and Exchange Ordinance 1969**, the **Bank Company Act 1991**, and the **Penal Code 1860**. Four regulators share jurisdiction: the ACC, the Bangladesh Financial Intelligence Unit (BFIU), the Bangladesh Securities and Exchange Commission (BSEC), and Bangladesh Bank.

## The Statutory Architecture: Five Statutes That Define White Collar Crime in Bangladesh

Most textbooks catalogue types of white collar crime without linking them to specific statutory provisions. That approach fails both in examinations and in court. What follows is a provision-level map of Bangladeshi white collar criminal law.

### The Penal Code 1860 as Foundation

The **Penal Code 1860** remains the residual criminal statute. Section 25 defines **"fraudulently"** as acting with intent to defraud. **Section 406** criminalises **criminal breach of trust** with imprisonment of three to seven years. **Section 415** defines **cheating**, carrying one to seven years' imprisonment depending on aggravating circumstances. Sections 421 through 424 address **fraudulent dispositions** of property to defeat creditors. **Section 463** criminalises **forgery** with imprisonment up to seven years. Prosecutors frequently charge Penal Code offences alongside specialised statutes to ensure fallback convictions.

### Money Laundering Prevention Act 2012

The **MLPA 2012** is Bangladesh's primary anti-laundering instrument. Section 4(2) prescribes four to twelve years' imprisonment plus a fine equal to double the asset value or BDT 2 million, whichever is greater. Section 4(3) mandates **state forfeiture** of laundered property. The Act enumerates 27 **predicate offences** in its schedule.

Section 12 imposes a critical procedural gate: no court may take cognizance of a money laundering offence without prior approval from the **BFIU**. This provision centralises prosecutorial control but also creates a bottleneck. Where BFIU approval is withheld or delayed, otherwise meritorious cases stall indefinitely.

### Anti-Corruption Commission Act 2004

The **ACC Act 2004** establishes the ACC as the sole nationwide authority to investigate scheduled corruption offences (s.20(1)). Section 26 empowers the ACC to issue **asset declaration notices** compelling disclosure of assets and liabilities. Section 32 requires a **prosecution sanction** (Form No. 3) before filing a charge sheet.

In *Tarique Rahman vs Government* (63 DLR (AD) 18), the Appellate Division held that MLPA offences constitute scheduled offences under ACC Act s.9, confirming the ACC's prosecution role over money laundering cases.

### Securities and Exchange Ordinance 1969

Orders 19 and 19A of the **Securities and Exchange Ordinance 1969** prohibit **insider trading**. Penalties reach up to five years' imprisonment or a fine of Tk 50 million, or both. For **fraudulent activities** in the securities market, the Ordinance prescribes up to five years' imprisonment or Tk 5 crore in fines, or both. BSEC exercises regulatory enforcement, but criminal prosecution requires referral to the criminal justice system. In 2019, BSEC fined Tk 2.29 crore on 16 persons and 3 firms, and suspended 3 stockbroker licences.

### Bank Company Act 1991

**Section 109** of the **Bank Company Act 1991** punishes banking offences with up to seven years' imprisonment and fines between Tk 5 lakh and Tk 50 lakh. The newer **section 27B** introduces **wilful defaulter** provisions: automatic vacancy of directorship, fines between Tk 50 lakh and Tk 1 crore, and a continuing default penalty of Tk 1 lakh per day.

## Landmark Cases: How Bangladeshi Courts Have Treated White Collar Offences

One white collar case produced life sentences. Another, involving $667 million in losses, produced zero prosecutions. The divergence reveals everything about Bangladeshi enforcement.

### The Hallmark-Sonali Bank Scam (Tk 3,500 Crore)

Between 2010 and 2012, Hallmark Group extracted approximately Tk 3,500 crore through fraudulent letter-of-credit-backed lending from Sonali Bank's Ruposhi Bangla branch. The scheme exploited fabricated export documentation and complicit bank officials.

In March 2024, a special tribunal delivered **life imprisonment** sentences for Hallmark Managing Director Tanvir Mahmud and his wife Jasmine Islam. Nine individuals were convicted in total, each fined Tk 50 million. The statutory basis comprised Penal Code s.406 (criminal breach of trust), s.420 (cheating), and the MLPA 2012.

This verdict represents the highest sentence ever imposed for a white collar offence in Bangladesh. It stands as proof that the statutory framework can produce serious consequences when political conditions permit prosecution.

### The 2010-11 Stock Market Scam

The Dhaka Stock Exchange General Index fell 1,800 points (21%) between December 2010 and January 2011, inflicting losses estimated at BDT 50 billion ($667 million) on retail investors. A government inquiry identified 60 individuals, including a former DSE president and a former SEC chairman.

Zero criminal prosecutions followed. Political protection shielded key actors, regulatory capture undermined BSEC independence, and no BFIU referral was initiated. The stock market scam remains Bangladesh's most consequential white collar crime with the least consequential enforcement response.

### ACC Procedural Jurisprudence

The Appellate Division has developed a body of procedural law constraining ACC overreach. In *Obaidul Kader vs State* (63 DLR 425), the court held that ACC discretionary powers must be exercised transparently; arbitrary action without recorded reasons violates s.3(2) of the ACC Act.

In *ACC vs Dr Mohiuddin Khan Alamgir* (62 DLR (AD) 290), the Appellate Division invalidated notices and mechanical sanctions, holding that s.26 asset declaration procedures must be properly followed. *ACC vs Shamima Begum* (62 DLR (AD) 277) established that abetment charges require substantive evidence, not prosecutorial assumption.

These decisions collectively hold that statutory tools exist but procedural compliance and political will determine whether they produce justice. Over 45% of significant white collar cases in Bangladesh experience unjustified prosecution delays, compounding the gap between law on the books and law in action.

## Criminal and Civil Remedies for White Collar Crime in Bangladesh

Criminal remedies punish. Civil remedies restore. Regulatory remedies prevent. Here is exactly where each lives in Bangladeshi law.

### Criminal Penalties

The penalty structure spans multiple statutes. The Penal Code prescribes one to seven years for cheating, criminal breach of trust, and forgery. MLPA 2012 s.4(2) imposes four to twelve years with mandatory forfeiture under s.4(3). The Securities Ordinance allows up to five years and Tk 5 crore in fines. Bank Company Act s.109 permits up to seven years.

All ACC-prosecuted offences require a prosecution sanction under s.32 (Form No. 3) before a charge sheet is filed. This procedural requirement, intended to prevent frivolous prosecution, has also served as a mechanism for political interference. Where sanction is withheld, otherwise complete investigations die on the file.

### Civil Remedies

The **Contract Act 1872** provides the primary civil framework. Section 17 defines **fraud** through five constituent acts: (1) false statement of fact, (2) active concealment of fact, (3) promise without intention of performing, (4) any other act fitted to deceive, and (5) any such act as law declares fraudulent. Section 19 renders contracts induced by fraud **voidable** at the option of the aggrieved party, with full restitution.

The **Specific Relief Act 1877** s.35 grants courts power to **rescind contracts** obtained through fraud. Civil damages in tort (the action for deceit) remain available. Victims may pursue **in rem** asset freezes alongside **in personam** damages claims, though jurisdictional complexity makes parallel proceedings expensive.

### Regulatory and Administrative Remedies

BSEC exercises administrative penalty powers including trading suspensions and deregistration of market intermediaries. The BFIU holds significant power under MLPA 2012 s.23(1)(c) to **freeze transactions without a court order**. Bangladesh Bank may revoke banking licenses and remove management. The ACC's s.26 asset declaration notice power functions as both investigative tool and administrative sanction.

### The Missing Remedy: Victim Compensation

Bangladesh has no dedicated **victim compensation fund** for white collar crime. A study conducted under the auspices of the United Nations Asia and Far East Institute for the Prevention of Crime (UNAFEI) concluded that current Bangladeshi law does not appropriately compensate victims. Victims must pursue ad-valorem fee-bearing civil suits while the state retains criminal fines.

This policy gap leaves retail investors who lost savings in the 2010-11 stock market crash, and depositors defrauded by banking scams, without direct redress. Criminal and civil tracks can run in parallel, but the cost of civil litigation effectively bars most victims from recovery.

## Corporate Liability for White Collar Offences Under Bangladeshi Law

A company cannot go to prison, but it can be fined, dissolved, and its directors personally convicted. The question is how Bangladeshi law bridges individual and corporate fault.

### The Companies Act 1994 Framework

**Part XI** of the **Companies Act 1994** criminalises **fraudulent trading**. Directors owe fiduciary duties as trustees of corporate assets, and breach carries both civil and criminal consequences. Courts retain the power to **pierce the corporate veil** when the company is used as a device for fraud. Acting as director while an undischarged insolvent carries up to 2 years' imprisonment or a fine up to Tk 5,000.

Bangladeshi courts overwhelmingly prefer individual prosecution over corporate sanctions. This preference reflects both doctrinal conservatism and practical difficulty in attributing criminal intent to an entity.

### Attribution of Criminal Liability

Bangladeshi courts apply the **"directing mind and will"** doctrine to attribute individual conduct to the corporation. The MLPA 2012 does not distinguish between natural and juristic persons; s.4 applies to **corporate persons**. Bank Company Act s.27B treats wilful default as grounds for automatic directorship vacancy. The ACC Act's reference to "any person" has been interpreted to include companies.

The statutory text permits corporate prosecution. The institutional practice avoids it. No reported case exists in which a Bangladeshi corporation, as distinct from its directors, received a criminal conviction for a white collar offence.

### The Doctrinal Gap

Bangladesh lacks equivalents to several international corporate liability mechanisms. There is no analogue to the UK Bribery Act s.7 ("failure to prevent" offence). **Deferred prosecution agreements** do not exist in Bangladeshi law. No **corporate sentencing guidelines** have been promulgated.

The Hallmark case illustrates the gap. Individuals were convicted and sentenced to life imprisonment, but the corporate structure that facilitated the fraud persisted. Related entities continued to operate. Until Bangladesh develops dedicated corporate criminal liability provisions, enforcement will remain person-focused and structurally incomplete. White collar crimes in Bangladesh will continue to be prosecuted against individuals while the corporate vehicles that enabled them survive intact.

## Enforcement Realities: Why White Collar Convictions Remain Rare in Bangladesh

The ACC's 2024 conviction rate of 48.17% was an eight-year low. With 3,410 cases pending and 965 stayed across court levels, the enforcement system is failing by its own metrics.

### The Numbers

In 2024, the ACC received 15,842 complaints and accepted 1,894, an acceptance rate of just 11.95%. The Commission filed 451 cases and submitted 403 charge sheets. The 48.17% conviction rate means that more than half of prosecuted cases ended in acquittal. Meanwhile, 3,410 cases sat pending across special tribunals, and 965 ACC cases were stayed by higher courts.

The BFIU reported stronger output in FY2024-25: 30,199 suspicious transaction reports and suspicious activity reports, a 74% increase over the prior year. The Unit froze assets worth Tk 76,000 crore. But freezing assets is not the same as convicting offenders or recovering stolen funds for victims.

### Structural Barriers

The nationwide case backlog stands at 4.65 million cases. Judicial independence is compromised by the Ministry of Law's control over postings and transfers of judges. Over 45% of significant white collar cases experience unjustified prosecution delays.

Approximately 90% of complaints filed with the ACC before August 2024 were abandoned without meaningful investigation. The ACC remains understaffed, and forensic accounting expertise is scarce. Complex financial crimes require specialist investigators that the ACC does not possess in sufficient numbers.

### The Post-Revolution Shift (August 2024)

Following the political transition of August 2024, the ACC filed 399 new cases against former Awami League figures. The surge in prosecution activity correlates with political change, not institutional reform. Global Financial Integrity's estimate of $150 billion in illicit transfers during the Hasina era provides the evidentiary backdrop.

This pattern warrants concern. Enforcement driven by regime change rather than institutional independence risks replicating the selective justice it purports to correct. The incoming government's political opponents face prosecution; its allies do not.

### BFIU as a Bright Spot

The BFIU's 30,199 STRs/SARs in FY2024-25 demonstrate growing financial intelligence capacity. The Tk 76,000 crore in frozen assets represents meaningful disruption of illicit financial flows. The BFIU's operational independence from direct political control distinguishes it from the ACC. As a member of the Egmont Group, an international network of over 170 financial intelligence units, the BFIU can exchange intelligence across borders.

However, financial intelligence without prosecutorial follow-through produces asset freezes, not convictions. The gap between BFIU output and courtroom outcomes remains the central enforcement failure in white collar crimes in Bangladesh.

## International Asset Recovery: UNCAC, Mutual Legal Assistance, and the $12 Billion Challenge

Transparency International Bangladesh's 2023 parallel report to the UNCAC review mechanism concluded that there had been "very little achievement in terms of asset recovery" despite estimated annual outflows exceeding $12 billion. Asset recovery is the ultimate test of a country's anti-corruption framework.

### The International Legal Framework

Bangladesh ratified the **United Nations Convention Against Corruption (UNCAC)** in 2007. Chapter V of UNCAC mandates asset recovery as a "fundamental principle" of the Convention. Bangladesh has concluded 81 bilateral memoranda of understanding for **mutual legal assistance** and is party to the South Asian MLAT Convention.

MLPA 2012 s.4(3) permits **forfeiture orders** that extend to foreign-held assets. The statutory authority to pursue international recovery exists on paper.

### Why Recovery Fails

Successive governments have lacked the political will to pursue predecessors' assets, because doing so established a precedent that would apply to their own conduct. **Shell companies** and layered ownership structures obscure **beneficial ownership** across multiple jurisdictions. Foreign financial institutions have repeatedly failed in their due diligence obligations.

**Dual criminality** requirements create additional barriers: the predicate offence must be criminal in both the requesting and requested state. Former Land Minister Saifuzzaman Chowdhury allegedly accumulated $675 million in assets across the United Kingdom, United States, Dubai, Malaysia, and Singapore. The dispersal of assets across multiple jurisdictions multiplies the legal complexity of recovery.

### The Path Forward

The UNCAC review mechanism has recommended that Bangladesh establish a **dedicated asset recovery office**. Reform of the Companies Act to require **beneficial ownership registries** would eliminate a key concealment mechanism. Bangladesh's membership in the **Egmont Group**, a network of over 170 financial intelligence units, facilitates intelligence exchange but requires operational strengthening.

**Civil forfeiture** (non-conviction-based asset recovery) offers a procedural alternative to criminal prosecution. It requires a lower evidentiary standard and does not depend on the presence or cooperation of the accused. Bangladesh's statute permits international asset recovery; its institutional capacity does not yet support it.

## Frequently Asked Questions: White Collar Crimes in Bangladesh

### What is the legal definition of white collar crime in Bangladesh?

No statutory definition exists. The term derives from Edwin Sutherland's 1939 criminological framing. Bangladeshi law addresses specific offences: fraud (Penal Code s.25), money laundering (MLPA 2012 s.4), securities fraud (Securities and Exchange Ordinance 1969), and corruption (ACC Act 2004). The category is doctrinal, not legislative.

### Which body investigates white collar crimes in Bangladesh?

The ACC holds sole nationwide authority for scheduled corruption offences under ACC Act s.20(1). The BFIU handles money laundering investigations. BSEC regulates securities fraud. Bangladesh Bank supervises banking offences under the Bank Company Act 1991. Jurisdiction depends on the offence charged.

### What is the maximum punishment for money laundering in Bangladesh?

MLPA 2012 s.4(2) prescribes four to twelve years' imprisonment plus a fine equal to double the laundered asset value or BDT 2 million, whichever is greater. Section 4(3) mandates state forfeiture of the laundered property. These penalties apply to both natural and juristic persons.

### Can a corporation be criminally liable in Bangladesh?

Yes. Companies Act 1994 Part XI criminalises fraudulent trading. The MLPA 2012 applies to juristic persons without distinction. However, corporate prosecutions are extremely rare in practice, with courts overwhelmingly preferring to prosecute individual directors and officers.

### What civil remedies are available to victims of white collar crime?

Victims may seek contract rescission under Specific Relief Act 1877 s.35, restitution under Contract Act 1872 s.19, and civil damages for deceit. BFIU transaction freezes under MLPA 2012 s.23(1)(c) can preserve assets pending litigation. No dedicated victim compensation fund exists in Bangladesh.

### Has Bangladesh recovered any assets from abroad?

Recovery has been minimal despite UNCAC ratification in 2007 and 81 bilateral mutual legal assistance agreements. Transparency International Bangladesh's 2023 report recorded "very little achievement" against estimated annual outflows exceeding $12 billion. Institutional capacity, political will, and jurisdictional complexity remain the primary obstacles.
