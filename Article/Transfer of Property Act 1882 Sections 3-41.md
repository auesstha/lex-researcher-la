# Transfer of Property Act 1882 Sections 3 to 41: A Simple Guide for Bangladesh Law Students

Most law students in Bangladesh jump from Section 3 (definitions) straight to Section 54 (sale). That means they skip 38 sections that examiners test every single year.

This guide covers those missing sections. You will learn who can transfer property, what cannot be transferred, void conditions, the rule against perpetuities, vested and contingent interests, and the ostensible owner rule. **Transfer of Property Act 1882 Section 3 to 41 Bangladesh** provisions form the entire foundation of property law in this country. The TPA 1882 applies in Bangladesh under the Laws Continuance Enforcement Order 1971, which continued all pre-independence laws after 1971. Every case cited below is a confirmed Bangladesh case.

## Section 3 of TPA 1882: Key Definitions You Must Know

Section 3 defines the terms used throughout the Act. If you understand these definitions, every later section becomes easier.

### Immovable Property

**Immovable property** means land, buildings, and anything permanently fixed to land. It does NOT include standing timber (trees that have not been cut yet), growing crops, or grass. Simple example: your family's house in Sylhet is immovable property. The mangoes growing on the tree in the yard are not.

### Attached to the Earth

**Attached to the earth** covers three situations. First, things rooted in the soil, like trees and shrubs. Second, things embedded in the earth, like buildings and boundary walls. Third, things permanently fastened to something that is embedded, like doors fitted into walls or ceiling fans bolted to the roof. A portable generator just sitting on the floor is NOT attached to the earth.

### Actionable Claim

An **actionable claim** is your right to recover a debt or a beneficial interest in movable property that is not in your possession. A court will enforce this right for you. Example: the money you deposited in a bank is an actionable claim. You can transfer your right to receive that money to someone else. But if you physically handed over your gold jewellery to a lender as security for a loan, that gold is NOT an actionable claim because it is already in someone else's possession.

In *Aloke Nath Dey v. Government of Bangladesh* (56 DLR AD 66), the Appellate Division held that ornaments held at financial institutions qualify as actionable claims because they represent unsecured debt.

### Notice

**Notice** means knowing something. There are two types. **Actual notice** means you directly know a fact. **Constructive notice** means the law says you should have known, even if you did not. Constructive notice arises in three ways under Section 3: (i) the relevant document was registered under the Registration Act, (ii) someone else was physically living on the property, or (iii) your agent knew about the fact.

### Registered

**Registered** means registered under the Registration Act 1908 as it applies in Bangladesh.

### Attested

**Attested** means a document signed by two or more witnesses. Each witness must have seen the person sign it (or heard the person confirm they signed it) and then signed it themselves. One key rule: attesting a document does not mean the witness knew what was written in it. The Appellate Division confirmed this in *Amanatullah v. Au Mohammad Bhuiyan* (17 BLD AD 199).

## Sections 4 to 9: What Is a Transfer, and Who Can Make One?

### Section 4: TPA and the Contract Act Work Together

The Transfer of Property Act must be read together with the **Contract Act 1872**. This means that to transfer property, you must first be able to enter a valid contract.

### Section 5: What Is a Transfer?

A **transfer of property** means a living person giving property to another living person, either now or in the future. This is called **inter vivos** (a Latin phrase meaning "between living people"). A will is NOT a transfer under the TPA. Wills are governed by the Succession Act 1925 or by personal law (for example, Muslim personal law for Muslims).

### Section 6: What Cannot Be Transferred

Not everything can be transferred. Section 6 lists the exceptions.

**Spes successionis** (hope of inheriting) cannot be transferred. Example: you cannot sell your expected share of your father's estate while your father is still alive.

A **right of re-entry** (the right to take back land if a condition is broken) cannot be transferred on its own.

An **easement** (the right to use someone else's land, like a right of way) cannot be transferred without the main land it serves.

A right to future **maintenance** (like alimony) cannot be transferred.

A **mere right to sue** cannot be transferred. You cannot sell a lawsuit to someone.

A public officer's salary or pension cannot be transferred.

Property that the law specifically forbids transferring also cannot be transferred. For example, vested property under the Vested Property Return Act 2001 cannot be transferred.

Exam tip: Section 6 is a list of EXCEPTIONS. Everything else can be transferred.

### Section 7: Who Can Transfer?

You must meet two conditions. First, you must be able to make a contract (above 18 years old, of sound mind). Second, you must be the owner of the property or authorized to transfer it. A **minor** (under 18) cannot transfer property. Under Section 11 of the Contract Act 1872, a minor's contract is void (completely empty, with no legal effect). A company can transfer property through its authorized officers.

### Section 8: What Passes with the Transfer?

When you sell your house, you automatically transfer everything attached to it. This includes fixtures, easements (like a right of way over a neighbour's land), rents, and profits. You do not need to mention these separately unless you want to exclude something specific.

### Section 9: Written or Spoken Transfer?

A transfer can be made verbally (by speaking) UNLESS the law requires it to be in writing. In Bangladesh, the Registration Act 1908 requires a written and registered deed for any immovable property worth more than Tk. 100. Since all property today is worth far more than Tk. 100, virtually every property transfer in Bangladesh must be in writing and registered.

## Sections 10, 11, and 12: Conditions That Are Void

These three sections share one idea: certain conditions attached to a transfer are legally meaningless. The condition is thrown out, but the transfer itself stays valid.

### Section 10: Absolute Restraint on Transfer Is Void

If you give someone property and tell them "you can never sell this to anyone," that condition is **void** (has no legal effect). The person gets the property and can sell it freely. However, partial restrictions may be valid. For example, "do not sell for five years" or "do not sell to person X" could be enforceable.

Exception: in a lease (renting), a no-transfer condition is valid. A lease is already a limited interest, so restricting further transfer is reasonable.

In *Sultan Ahmed v. Md. Waziullah* (7 BLD HCD 235), the High Court Division held that giving property for life with a restriction on transfer does not violate Section 10. A life interest is already limited, so the restriction does not amount to an absolute restraint.

### Section 11: Condition Contradicting the Interest Is Void

If you give A land with full ownership (absolutely) but then say "A cannot mortgage it," that restriction is void. A already has full ownership. A condition that contradicts full ownership is meaningless.

In *Asim Ali v. Badaruddin* (46 DLR 96), the court held that Section 11 only applies to absolute interests. An **ekrarnama** (an agreement to sell) does not pass absolute interest, so Section 11 does not apply to it. The court also confirmed that a right of repurchase is non-transferable.

### Section 12: Insolvency Condition Is Void

You cannot say "if the person I gave this property to ever goes bankrupt, the property returns to me." That condition is void. The reason is simple: it would cheat the bankrupt person's creditors, who have a legal right to the property.

Exam tip: In all three sections (10, 11, 12), the condition is void but the transfer itself remains valid. The transferee keeps the property.

## Sections 13 to 18: Transfer to an Unborn Child and the Rule Against Perpetuities

### Section 13: Transfer to an Unborn Child

You cannot directly give property to a child who is not yet born. Instead, you must follow two steps. First, create a **life interest** (the right to use property during one's lifetime) for a person who is already alive. Second, pass the remainder to the unborn child when born, and the unborn child must receive the FULL interest, not a limited one.

Valid example: "I give this land to my wife for her life, and after she dies, absolutely to my unborn child." Invalid example: "I give this land to my wife for her life, and then to my unborn child for their life only." The second example fails because the unborn child receives only a life interest, not the full interest.

### Section 14: The Rule Against Perpetuities

Property cannot be tied up forever. The **rule against perpetuities** says that an interest must **vest** (become definite and fixed) within the lifetime of a living person at the time of the transfer, PLUS the minority of the final beneficiary.

In Bangladesh, the formula is: life in being + minority (age 18, under the Majority Act 1875). This is different from English law, which adds 21 years instead of minority.

Simple example: "To A for life, then to A's first son when he turns 18." This is valid. "To A for life, then to A's first son when he turns 25." This could be void because 25 exceeds the minority threshold of 18.

In *Chandra Kanta Mistri v. Sailendra Nath Sikder* (49 DLR 514), the court held that the rule against perpetuity applies even when no specific repayment timeframe is mentioned.

In *Abdul Quddus v. Anjuman Khatun* (4 BLD HCD 237), the court clarified that the rule against perpetuity does not apply to contracts of sale.

### Section 16: Class of People

If you give property to a group (for example, "all my grandchildren who reach 18"), those members of the group who cannot satisfy the condition are excluded. Those who can still take their share.

### Section 18: Public Benefit Exemption

The perpetuity rule does NOT apply to transfers made for the public good. This covers religion, education, health, commerce, and public safety. This is why **waqf** (Islamic endowment) properties in Bangladesh are not subject to this rule. **Waqf-al-aulad** (family waqf) is especially common in Bangladesh and benefits from this exemption.

## Sections 19 to 30: Vested Interest and Contingent Interest

This is one of the most tested topics in Bangladesh LLB exams. Understand the difference clearly, and you will answer exam questions with confidence.

### Section 19: Vested Interest

A **vested interest** is an interest that is fixed and certain. No future condition needs to happen for you to get it. Even if you have to wait to enjoy it, you already own it legally. And if you die before you can enjoy it, your heirs get it.

Example: "I give this land to A when A turns 25." From the moment of the transfer, A has a vested interest. If A dies at age 20, A's heirs will get the land when A would have turned 25.

### Section 21: Contingent Interest

A **contingent interest** depends on something uncertain happening. If you die before that uncertain thing happens, the interest is lost. It does not pass to your heirs.

Example: "I give this land to A if A marries B." If A dies without marrying B, the interest is gone. Nobody gets the land from A's side.

### Comparison Table

| Feature | Vested Interest (Section 19) | Contingent Interest (Section 21) |
|---|---|---|
| Is there an uncertain condition? | No | Yes |
| What happens if the person dies first? | Interest passes to heirs | Interest is lost |
| Can it be transferred? | Yes, freely | Yes, but subject to the condition |
| Nature of the right | Definite ownership | Mere hope or expectancy |

### Section 20: Unborn Child's Interest

If property is set aside for an unborn child, the child gets a vested interest as soon as they are born.

### Section 22: Class Gifts

If you give to "all my children who reach 18," only the children who actually reach 18 get a share. A child who dies before turning 18 gets nothing.

### Sections 23 to 30: Conditional Transfers

These sections deal with variations of conditional transfers. The key principle stays the same. If a condition must happen first (this is called a **condition precedent**), the interest is contingent until it does. If the condition can end an existing interest (this is called a **condition subsequent**), the interest is vested until the condition occurs.

## Sections 31 to 40: Special Rules on Transfers

### Section 31: Condition Subsequent

A transfer can be subject to a condition that will end it if something happens. Example: "I give this land to A, but if A moves abroad permanently, the land returns to me."

If the condition is too unreasonable, courts will strike it down. A condition precedent (Sections 25 to 29) must happen BEFORE the interest vests. A condition subsequent ends an interest that has already vested.

### Section 35: The Doctrine of Election

Sometimes a transfer deed gives a benefit to one person AND tries to give away property belonging to a different person. In that case, the person whose property is being given away must **elect** (choose).

They can either: (a) accept the benefit given to them under the deed and let their property go, or (b) reject the benefit and keep their own property. They cannot do both.

They have one year to decide after learning about the transfer.

Example: X's deed transfers Y's house to Z, but the same deed gives Y Tk. 50,000. Y must choose. Take the Tk. 50,000 and let the house go to Z, or reject the Tk. 50,000 and keep the house.

### Section 37: Apportionment

If mortgaged land is divided among several owners, each owner is responsible for their proportional share of the mortgage debt.

### Section 38: Limited Owner Transfer

A person who only has a limited interest (like a **life tenant**, someone who owns property only for their lifetime) can only transfer what they have. They cannot give someone more than they themselves own. Any transfer beyond their limited interest is **voidable** (can be set aside by a court) but not automatically void if the buyer acted in good faith.

### Section 39: Maintenance Rights

If someone is legally entitled to receive maintenance from the income of a property, that right survives when the property is transferred to a new owner. The new owner must honour it, as long as they had notice of it. A **bona fide buyer** (genuine buyer) without notice is not bound.

### Section 40: Restrictive Covenants

An obligation on how land can be used (for example, "this land shall only be used for residential purposes") binds any subsequent owner who had notice of it. Because the Registration Act 1908 gives constructive notice of all registered documents, a restriction written into a registered deed will bind all future buyers.

## Section 41: Buying from the Wrong Person, the Ostensible Owner Rule

This is a favourite examination topic. Read it carefully.

An **ostensible owner** is someone who appears to be the owner of property. The real owner has, intentionally or by staying silent, allowed this other person to act as if they own the property. "Ostensible" simply means "appearing to be."

### The Rule

If you buy property from someone who APPEARS to be the owner, and you are careful and honest about it, the real owner cannot take the property back from you. This is true even if the seller was not the true owner.

### Five Conditions (All Must Be Satisfied)

1. The seller must be an ostensible owner (appears to be the owner to the world).
2. The real owner must have consented, either expressly (said yes) or by passive acquiescence (silently allowed it). See *Abdul Quddus v. Anjuman Khatun* (4 BLD HCD 237).
3. The transfer must be for **consideration** (payment). Gifts are NOT protected under Section 41.
4. The buyer must have taken **reasonable care** to check the seller's title (looked at documents, checked land records).
5. The buyer must have acted in **good faith** (honestly, with no ulterior motive). In *Abdul Gafur v. Ali Akbar* (4 BLD HCD 326), the court held that a buyer who failed to examine title deeds was not acting in good faith.

### Who Carries the Burden of Proof?

The buyer must prove all five conditions. The real owner does not have to prove anything. This was confirmed in *Wahida Begum v. Tajul Islam* (52 DLR 491).

### Benami Transactions

In Bangladesh, land is sometimes registered in a relative's name. This is called a **benami** transaction. The person whose name appears in the records is the **benamdar** (nominee). Section 41 issues arise frequently in these cases. In *Sultan Ahmed v. Waziullah* (39 DLR 329), the court held that a buyer from a benamdar is protected only if both good faith and reasonable care are proven.

## Bangladesh's Own Rules: The 2004 and 2026 Amendments

### How TPA Came to Bangladesh

The TPA 1882 was a British Indian law. When Bangladesh became independent in 1971, all existing laws continued under the Laws Continuance Enforcement Order 1971. In 1973, Bangladesh updated terminology: "Pakistan" became "Bangladesh," "Rs." became "Tk.," and "Muhammadan" became "Muslim."

### Why Bangladesh Amended the TPA

India also inherited the TPA 1882 but took a different path of amendments. Bangladesh made its own changes to fix real problems in Bangladesh land transactions. These Bangladesh amendments have no equivalent in Indian law.

### The Four 2004 Rules (Sections 53B to 53E)

**Section 53B: No double selling.** If you have already signed a contract to sell your property, you cannot sell it to someone else while the contract is still running. If you do, that second sale is void.

**Section 53C: Khatian requirement.** You can only sell property if your name (or your predecessor's name, for inherited land) appears in the latest **khatian** (the official land record under the State Acquisition and Tenancy Act, 1950). No valid khatian entry means the sale is void. This is why sellers in Bangladesh must first complete **namjari** (mutation, meaning updating the khatian in their name) before selling.

**Section 53D: Mortgaged property needs lender's permission.** If your property is under a registered mortgage (with a bank or lender), you cannot sell or re-mortgage it without the lender's written permission. No permission means the transfer is void.

**Section 53E: Affidavit requirement.** Every deed, whether for sale, gift, mortgage, or **heba** (Islamic gift), must be accompanied by an **affidavit** (a sworn written statement) from the transferor confirming they legally own the property.

### The 2026 Amendment: Giving Property but Keeping Its Benefits (Sections 122A and 122B)

Bangladesh's 2026 Amendment Act (Act No. 105 of 2026) introduced a completely new kind of gift. You can now give your property to a child, grandchild, or spouse but keep the right to live on it and enjoy its income during your lifetime. This right is called **usufruct** (the right to use and enjoy property that belongs to someone else).

This only works between close family members: parent to child or grandchild (or the reverse), or between spouses.

After the donor (giver) dies, the property goes to the donee (receiver). If the donee dies first, the property goes to the donee's heirs.

Once registered, this gift cannot be taken back except in two situations. First, by mutual written agreement in a registered deed, where there is genuine financial, medical, educational, or family hardship. Second, by order of the District Judge where the donee cannot consent (for example, because they are a minor or mentally incapacitated).

This law was introduced to protect elderly parents who want to secure their children's inheritance rights while still being able to live on the property during their lifetime.

## FAQ

### What is the difference between vested interest and contingent interest?

A vested interest is fixed and certain from the moment of transfer. If the holder dies, heirs inherit it. A contingent interest depends on an uncertain event. If the holder dies before that event happens, the interest is lost forever. Vested interests can be transferred freely. Contingent interests can also be transferred, but they remain subject to the uncertain condition.

### Does the Transfer of Property Act 1882 still apply in Bangladesh?

Yes. The Laws Continuance Enforcement Order 1971 kept all pre-independence laws in force. The TPA 1882 continues to govern property transfers in Bangladesh. Bangladesh amended it in 1973 (terminology changes), 2004 (Sections 53B to 53E on land fraud prevention), and 2026 (Sections 122A and 122B on gifts with retained usufruct).

### What is the rule against perpetuities in Bangladesh, and how is it different from English law?

The rule says property cannot be locked away from transfer forever. An interest must vest within the lifetime of a person alive at the time of transfer, plus the minority of the beneficiary (age 18 under the Majority Act 1875). English law uses "life in being plus 21 years." Bangladesh uses "life in being plus minority." This makes the Bangladesh rule stricter for beneficiaries older than 18.

### What are the five conditions for Section 41 (ostensible owner) to protect a buyer?

The seller must appear to be the owner. The real owner must have consented or stayed silent. The transfer must be for payment (not a gift). The buyer must have checked the seller's title with reasonable care. The buyer must have acted in good faith. All five must be proven by the buyer, as confirmed in *Wahida Begum v. Tajul Islam* (52 DLR 491).

### What is khatian, and why does Section 53C of the Bangladesh TPA require it?

A **khatian** is the official record of land ownership maintained under the State Acquisition and Tenancy Act, 1950. Section 53C (added in 2004) requires that a seller's name, or their predecessor's name for inherited property, must appear in the latest khatian before any sale. Without this, the sale is void. This rule was introduced to prevent people from selling land they do not actually own.
