# Is It Possible to Get Relief from Debt by Declaring Bankruptcy in Bangladesh?

In twenty-six years since the **Bankruptcy Act 1997** took effect, Bangladesh has seen only 533 to 638 bankruptcy petitions filed across the entire country. Between 2016 and 2020, courts disposed of exactly two cases out of eleven filed. The statute does provide a pathway to debt discharge, and this article walks through every relevant provision. But the near-total dormancy of this system forces a harder question: can a debtor in Bangladesh actually obtain meaningful relief through bankruptcy? The statutory framework, case data, the unresolved conflict with Artha Rin Adalat proceedings, and the proposed Insolvency and Bankruptcy Ordinance 2025 all reveal an answer far more complicated than the statute suggests.

## The Short Answer: Statutory Relief Exists but Remains Practically Elusive

Yes, the Bankruptcy Act 1997 provides for adjudication of bankruptcy and eventual discharge. A debtor whose debts exceed Tk. 20,000 can file a voluntary petition under Section 13. Creditors holding at least Tk. 5,00,000 in unsecured debt can file after a debtor commits an **act of bankruptcy** under Section 9, most commonly by failing to satisfy a statutory demand within 90 days.

Upon adjudication, Section 31 vests all of the bankrupt's property in the **Official Receiver** automatically. The court imposes a moratorium. Section 47 then provides the mechanism for **discharge**, which can be absolute or conditional.

Discharge is extraordinarily rare in practice. The case of Apurna-Zahid Fashion Limited, filed in August 2000, was still pending as of 2021. Over twenty years without resolution. Sections 44 and 48 impose a 50% recovery threshold as one ground for refusing discharge. The debtors most desperately in need of a fresh start are the least likely to obtain one.

The system also operates on a critical structural divide. The Bankruptcy Act 1997 governs individuals and partnerships. Corporate insolvency falls under the Companies Act 1994 winding-up provisions. These are entirely separate regimes with different courts, different procedures, and different outcomes.

## Statutory Framework Under the Bankruptcy Act 1997

Section 9 defines the **acts of bankruptcy** that trigger creditor petitions: absconding to avoid debts, making a fraudulent transfer of property, giving written notice of inability to pay, or suffering execution against property. The most common trigger is a debtor's failure to comply with a creditor's statutory demand for Tk. 5,00,000 or more within 90 days.

A debtor can also self-petition under Section 13, with a much lower threshold of Tk. 20,000 in debt. The petition goes to the **District Court** sitting as the Bankruptcy Court. After examination, the court issues an **Order of Adjudication**, published in the official Gazette.

Section 32 exempts certain property from the bankruptcy estate: tools of trade, household necessities, and a homestead of up to 2,500 square feet in urban areas. The total exemption is capped at Tk. 3,00,000. Legislators have never adjusted this cap for inflation since 1997, and in real terms the exemption has eroded dramatically.

Section 47 governs **discharge**. The court may grant absolute or conditional discharge after considering the bankrupt's conduct and the creditors' recovery. Section 51 carves out **non-dischargeable debts**: government debts, debts arising from fraud "to which [the bankrupt] was a party," and maintenance orders.

Section 28 allows defendants in bankruptcy suits to apply for dismissal on grounds including solvency. The Appellate Division confirmed in *Emdadul Haque Bhuivan v. Bankruptcy Court*, 8 BLT (AD)-175 that a guarantor cannot obtain dismissal under Section 28(b) simply by virtue of their guarantor status. They must affirmatively prove capacity to repay and that they are not a willful defaulter.

Section 94 imposes severe restrictions on an **undischarged bankrupt**: no bank loans, disqualification from Parliament, the judiciary, and public office. These restrictions persist indefinitely until the court grants discharge.

Section 60 provides a **claw-back period** of 15 years. Courts can reverse any transfers made within this window. The United Kingdom applies a two-year look-back; India's IBC 2016 also uses two years. Bangladesh's 15-year provision deters voluntary filing.

Section 43 offers an alternative through a **composition or scheme of arrangement**. This requires approval by creditors holding two-thirds of the total debt value, plus court sanction. A successful composition can annul the adjudication order entirely, but this provision sees minimal use in practice.

## Why the System Barely Functions: Practical Barriers to Bankruptcy Relief

The numbers tell the story. Only 533 to 638 total bankruptcy petitions were filed in the 26 years since the Act took effect. Between 2016 and 2020, eleven cases were filed. Two were disposed of. Approximately Tk. 2,600 crore remains stuck in pending cases. As of June 2023, 172 cases were still under trial, with banks claiming Tk. 4.24 billion.

Banks avoid the system entirely. Bangladesh Bank spokesperson Md Mezbaul Haque stated plainly: "The banks are not considering going to the bankruptcy courts in good numbers to file the cases as they deem it as not viable or effective option to recover the default loan." Barrister Khan Mohammad Shamim Aziz put it more bluntly: "Banks always lose in bankruptcy suits in the sense that they cannot recover default loans."

From the debtor's side, the incentives are equally discouraging. The 50% recovery threshold under Sections 44 and 48 means a deeply insolvent debtor, one who cannot pay half of what is owed, faces denial of discharge. The Tk. 3,00,000 exemption cap set in 1997 has not been revised despite decades of inflation. The 15-year claw-back under Section 60 exposes virtually every significant transaction a debtor has ever made.

Bangladesh's **non-performing loan** ratio reached 36% by September 2025, with Tk. 6.44 lakh crore in distressed bank debt. Total distressed assets climbed to approximately Tk. 11 lakh crore by early 2026, roughly 60% of banking sector loans. Yet the bankruptcy system resolves virtually nothing. Only one corporate bankruptcy was declared in the last decade.

## The Artha Rin Adalat Conflict and the Strategic Defaulter Loophole

Bangladesh operates a dual-track system for debt resolution that creates a dangerous gap. The **Artha Rin Adalat Act 2003** governs loan recovery by banks and financial institutions through specialized **Money Loan Courts**. The Bankruptcy Act 1997 governs collective insolvency proceedings. No statutory coordination mechanism exists between the two.

Banks overwhelmingly prefer the Artha Rin Adalat. Section 12 of that Act permits out-of-court enforcement against secured property. Section 34 authorizes civil imprisonment for up to six months. Section 42 requires a 75% appeal deposit, discouraging challenges. These are aggressive creditor tools.

Research published on ResearchGate analyzing the effort to rescue Bangladesh's Bankruptcy Act 1997 found a critical loophole: if a debtor files a bankruptcy petition while Artha Rin Adalat proceedings are pending, the Artha Rin Adalat suit automatically becomes invalid. Courts have not authoritatively resolved which regime prevails when the two collide. This gap is exploitable.

The High Court Division addressed the boundary between the two regimes in *Shamshad Asif v. Additional District Judge*, HCD, 52 DLR 138 (1999). Al-Baraka Bank had obtained Artha Rin Adalat decrees against the petitioner and simultaneously filed bankruptcy proceedings. The court held that bankruptcy suits proceed independently of decree proceedings; the nature and issues of each forum differ fundamentally. Crucially, the court confirmed that res judicata does not bar a bankruptcy petition simply because an Artha Rin Adalat decree already exists against the same debtor. The two regimes serve different purposes and neither forecloses the other.

The Artha Rin framework has its own enforcement vulnerabilities. In *Dr. Kazi Sirazul Islam v. Artha Rin Adalat No. 1, Dhaka*, HCD, 2022(1) LNJ 263 (2023), the court addressed Section 12(3) of the Artha Rin Adalat Act 2003, which conditions subsequent suit-filing on a prior failed auction attempt. A mortgagor had obtained a stay, frustrated the auction, and then argued the bank had "failed" to sell within the statutory meaning. The court rejected this. Obstruction engineered by the mortgagor through a meritless writ petition cannot satisfy the statutory "failure" requirement. Banks may restart enforcement once such stays are vacated.

The practical result is devastating. Large **strategic defaulters**, responsible for much of the Tk. 6.44 lakh crore NPL crisis, face neither effective recovery proceedings through the Artha Rin Adalat nor meaningful insolvency consequences through the Bankruptcy Act. They occupy a zone of impunity between two broken systems.

For practitioners advising bank creditors, forum selection must account for the invalidation risk. A borrower's voluntary bankruptcy petition can frustrate bank recovery proceedings, but it triggers automatic vesting of all assets in the Official Receiver under Section 31.

## Corporate Insolvency: The Companies Act 1994 Winding-Up Path

The Bankruptcy Act 1997 does not apply to corporations. It covers individuals and partnerships only. Corporate insolvency is governed exclusively by **Part IX of the Companies Act 1994** (Sections 234 through 308), which provides for winding-up proceedings before the High Court Division sitting as the **Companies Bench**.

Section 241 establishes the primary ground relevant here: inability to pay debts. A creditor petition triggers the appointment of an **Official Liquidator**, who realizes the company's assets and distributes proceeds according to statutory priority.

In practice, this regime is almost entirely unused for insolvency purposes. Only one corporate winding-up on insolvency grounds has been declared in the last decade. The process is slow, expensive, and offers no rescue mechanism.

Bangladesh has no equivalent to India's **Corporate Insolvency Resolution Process** under the IBC 2016, with its time-bound 180-day resolution period (extendable by 90 days). There is no equivalent to the United States' **Chapter 11** reorganization. There is no debtor-in-possession financing framework. Section 46 of the Bankruptcy Act, which theoretically provides for reorganization of non-individual debtors, has been described as a "dead letter" in LegalSeba's analysis. It has never been successfully used.

Bangladesh has also not adopted the **UNCITRAL Model Law on Cross-Border Insolvency**. Foreign creditors have no statutory framework for participation, and assets held abroad fall outside the reach of domestic proceedings.

## The Proposed Insolvency and Bankruptcy Ordinance 2025: Reform on the Horizon

A draft **Insolvency and Bankruptcy Ordinance 2025** has been prepared under the interim government with involvement from BAIRA (Bangladesh Association of Insolvency Restructuring and Administration). As of October 2026, it has not been enacted as operative law. The Chambers Banking Regulation 2026 guide references a proposed "Insolvency and Bankruptcy Act 2026," suggesting possible enactment under a revised title.

The draft Ordinance represents a paradigm shift. Its primary objective is **going-concern rescue**, moving away from the current liquidation-first approach. Creditor value optimization is positioned as a secondary objective. The proposals include cross-border insolvency provisions, accelerated reorganization procedures, a dedicated **Insolvency Tribunal**, and a regulatory body analogous to India's **Insolvency and Bankruptcy Board of India (IBBI)**.

A separate but related development: the **Bank Resolution Ordinance 2025** was enacted in May 2025 and granted Bangladesh Bank authority to intervene in failing banks. This led to the forced merger of five Islamic banks into Sammilito Islami Bank PLC in September 2025. That ordinance addresses systemic banking resolution, not general insolvency for individual or corporate debtors.

The direct driver of reform is the NPL crisis. With a 36% non-performing loan ratio and total distressed assets approaching Tk. 11 lakh crore by early 2026, the status quo is untenable. Significant implementation uncertainties remain. Bangladesh has no trained cadre of insolvency professionals, political transition risks cloud the legislative timeline, and the banking sector may resist a framework that forces collective proceedings over bilateral recovery.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is the minimum debt threshold to file a bankruptcy petition in Bangladesh?

It depends on who files. A creditor must hold at least Tk. 5,00,000 in unsecured debt and demonstrate an act of bankruptcy under Section 9 of the Bankruptcy Act 1997, most commonly by issuing a statutory demand that goes unsatisfied for 90 days. A debtor can file a voluntary petition under Section 13 with debts as low as Tk. 20,000.

### Can a company declare bankruptcy under the Bankruptcy Act 1997?

No. The Bankruptcy Act 1997 applies only to individuals and partnerships. Corporate insolvency is governed by Part IX of the Companies Act 1994 (Sections 234 through 308), which provides for winding-up proceedings before the High Court Division. No corporate rescue or reorganization mechanism equivalent to India's IBC or US Chapter 11 currently exists.

### How long does the bankruptcy process take in Bangladesh?

No statutory time limit applies. In practice, cases routinely take 10 to 20 years or more. The Apurna-Zahid Fashion Limited case was filed in August 2000 and remained pending as of 2021. Between 2016 and 2020, only 2 out of 11 filed cases were disposed of. The system offers no meaningful timeline for resolution.

### What property is exempt from the bankruptcy estate?

Section 32 of the Bankruptcy Act 1997 exempts tools of trade, personal effects, household necessities, and a homestead of up to 2,500 square feet in urban areas. The total value of exempt property is capped at Tk. 3,00,000. This cap has not been revised since 1997, making it severely inadequate in current economic conditions.

### Will the proposed 2025 Ordinance change the bankruptcy system?

If enacted, the draft Insolvency and Bankruptcy Ordinance 2025 would introduce time-bound resolution, a dedicated Insolvency Tribunal, corporate rescue procedures, a fresh-start provision, and cross-border insolvency mechanisms. As of October 2026, the Ordinance remains in draft form and has not been enacted into law. Implementation will depend on political will and institutional capacity building.
